I'll talk about this project I did with the Stuart Hall Archives at the University of Birmingham, where I’m grateful to have worked with Rebecca Roach as a visiting researcher.

This project is broadly interested in the question "How did words become vectors?" Not just numbers but spatialised probabilities, reducing meaning to measurable comparisons and arrows in latent space? 

It's interested in how the technical choices that create numerical representations come to inform large generative text outputs — and how historical developments come to inform those technical choices. Both how those historical developments are represented and historicized and their potential alternative narratives that exist in parallel, for ways they might have or might still create alternative futures. 

As an extension it's asking about the current AI accelerations we see, where they require reductions in meaning making and in modes of representation of identity making, where they work to transmit and amplify power and ideology, how they do that work.

I have been interested in how cultural studies and other perspectives taken up from outside a white western canon might have differently informed a cybernetics in formation and by extension an AI discourse in formation. 


With that in mind I came to the Stuart Hall Archives in Birmingham to look at drafts of Hall's essay Encoding/Decoding. 

I did an archive visit funded by the archive project in Feb of this year, in which I focused on Hall’s drafts of Encoding/Decoding and any references related to cybernetics



Started by compiling the various subtle variations of the diagram found in versions of Hall's Encoding/Decoding essay drafts.

As well as connecting the ideas found there to Hall's intellectual history in other works around cultural studies and diasporic identity. 

Plus I looked for any references to cybernetics or early computing.

Some things to note:
- Hall focuses on meaning in this diagram, and how it moves. 
- For Hall, he says that 'meaning is made in practices'
- He highlights this moves as a circuit (connects to his later collaborative work on cultural circuits)
- And he sees the transmission of meaning as always imbued with and reflective of practices of ideology and expressive of power dynamics




My initial impetus for working with Hall and Cybernetics was the contrast of that diagram with Claude Shannon's famous diagram that launched the field of Information theory. 

It studies how information can be relayed as a signal from a source to a target, by a transmitter. And it accounts for noise that might interfere. 

Shannon explicitly sets aside the content or meaning of the message as unimportant to his analysis, for the purposes of studying signal transmission.

And this move is perhaps fundamental to a kind of mindset seen in many approaches in what would become the field of computer science from the mid 20th century onward.

Look at the Spacialization of language — we may take for granted this element now, but I am interested in how this came to be. 

In natural language processing, it is often attributed to this oft repeated quote from 1957 from JR Firth, "the meaning of a word can be inferred by the company it keeps." And this remains a foundational principle of language-based ML today.

One of the first places we see this applied is in work by Osgood from 1957, where he maps language to a spatial, cartesian plane. 


Become the contemporary conceptualizations of 'latent space' with which computer scientists imagine a secret world of language and meaning spatialized and navigable in points between words-as-vectors, where king equals queen minus woman plus man.

In 1960 Linguist Roman Jakobson who was close with the cyberneticists presents this model of communication theory that appears a riff on Shannon's but with message included (still not meaning.)




Also in 1960, Communication studies was developing as a field at the same time as computer science, both in some ways out of cybernetics, and also got in on the diagraming of the transmission of content or meaning. 

Here we see encoding-decoding language of computation appear (not necessarily for the first time), and it acknowledges a mass culture element of communication — that it does not happen just between individuals but is influenced by social and cultural forces.


This is an example of another archive I worked with previously, the Joseph Weizenbaum papers at MIT Archives. His papers include the original source code for the early chatbot ELIZA, as well as this diagram describing the flow that the software should take. It includes input and procedural processing of text, and a capacity for short term memory storage that gave a more robust illusion of verisimilitude. 

With the divide into two parts, a reader system and response system, which is not quite how the system ended up being built in practice, the diagram echoes many of the other diagrams here that include encoder and decoder or similar transformation rules.



This is Darton's communications circuit that appears in 1982, which attends to the production of writing — manufacturing and editorial processes. It includes the medium of text and the culture around it — but again not the content of the book itself or any meaning it might contain (especially in relation to anything else)


By 1997 Cultural studies is well established and presents methods of 'close reading' mass media and its objects, and this circuit of culture thinks through how a transformative media object and physical technology like the Sony Walkman also shapes users' sense of identity in relation to it as well as is shaped by its manufacturer's processes and so forth.
