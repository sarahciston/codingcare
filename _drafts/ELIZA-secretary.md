Like other scholars, including Rebecca Roach (2024) who even tracked down members of the HR department to no avail, our search for the person behind the myth of Weizenbaum’s secretary has so far been a failure. 

In a copy of a typed letter from Weizenbaum to the telephone company, we find the initials "JW:ky" — yet "ky" matches none of the initials of the administrative staff for Project MAC. Even allowing for possible name changes, reassignments, or a shared secretarial pool, we were nowhere. Another dead end. 

Why does this matter? Isn't it a tangent? 

In striving to name the secretary, we aim for a generous citational practice. We hope to include all those (who wish to be named) and how their roles contributed to the story of ELIZA and its impact. Knowledge that comes from outside traditional academic pathways often goes unnoted. Questioning those pathways, and supplementing them with digs in the archives and lateral thinking, can help expand accepted forms of knowledge.

Weizenbaum’s oft-repeated anecdote includes how present his secretary was present throughout the development of ELIZA, and he notes how much her reaction shapes his own research concerns from then on. Her identity as a key character in this story becomes a gap in our knowledge. We know Bobrow

it does become a gap. It also feels weird to me to cite by name everyone except for the people who are likely women. Given what Janet had to say about the culture of MIT at the time, if it were a grad student or research assistant instead of personal assistant, I wonder if they would have been named as a participant in the project. I wouldn't imagine it to be any kind of gotcha moment, or to make a big deal about it, I hope not, but rather for parity with the rest of the sources and citations.

Secondly, I think there's an aspect of what Weizenbaum warned of the anthropomorphizing of systems that gets gender coded, mixed up with ELIZA as an agent, and tied into this. Certainly the "young lady's" "my boyfriend made me come here" and the "secretary's" request for "intimacy" are female-coded in a way that the rest is not, so it is also in the interest of having these aspects not go nameless in order to detooth that a bit, in a tech history space where this is too often the case. I do think our book is about this, especially Weizenbaum's entanglement with this question of people's reaction to computational 'agents', and the secretary story is the start of this myth.

MIT’s CSAIL department celebrated 50 years of Project MAC with a photo gallery on their website. The archive captions identify the women pictured as, “Need Name” or “Name?”, or sometimes “Halloween Party 1963.” When alone these women “Need Name” and when alongside men their presence is not mentioned at all. Only four women are named, and this is what I could learn of them. Julie Mazel (Academic Staff, Research Assistant, wife of Gerald Jay Sussman, would later contribute to his Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs), Harriet Fell (now Professor of CS at Northeastern), Maryanne/Marianne Henneman (Non-Academic Research Staff investigating use for Library and Periodical Search/Sorting), and Lucy Darley (unknown). Only three men are left as “Need Name” and only four women are not “Need Name.” and only three men are Need Name. Men are identified with high confidence even when their heads are cut off, their backs are turned, or a photo is of only their hand. Equipment is identified. Photos are of moments at work and on breaks, office Halloween parties and late nights. One woman is identified by the name on her going-away-party cake, although of course her cake does not include a last name. 

Consider: Klára Dán von Neumann was an ENIAC programmer who helped implement the first operating systems, developing the division between 'background coding' and 'program coding', as well as developing the first computational versions of the Monte Carlo method. Her research led to complex calculations still used today in COVID research. Her husband John is also well known in the history of computation. [^vonNeumann]

Harriet Fell

A professor at Northeastern University’s College of Computer Science, Harriet Fell competed in STS in 1961. As a freshman at MIT, she was one of only 23 women in a class of about 900 students. “In one math class, a male student told the professor he was upset because I was better than he was. The professor assured him that it didn’t matter because I would drop out to get married and have babies while he would still do mathematics,” said Harriet. “I was always good at math and sciences. I was really bad at French and history. I hated subjects where I had to memorize lots of stuff. I don’t know why I never felt like I had to memorize a lot for science subjects. All those science words somehow stuck without much effort.”
