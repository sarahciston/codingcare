# Go On, Now: What Future Language Models Can Learn from ELIZA

“absolute understanding on the part of either humans or machines is impossible.” (Weizenbaum 1967)

Generative AI and large language models (LLMs) are having their own "ELIZA effect” moment, and ELIZA is both a promise and a harbinger for what’s to come. This chapter examines LLMs alongside ELIZA, comparing some of their foundational concepts, structures, and technical processes. It focuses on the misattribution of intelligence and complexity in both systems that belies their comparative simplicity. Then it addresses how Weizenbaum's critique of computation applies to emerging machine learning practices, and how it points to alternatives for AI systems.[^1]  
As discussed in §2.5, many of the most popular LLMs have relied on chatbot interfaces to gather cultural cachet. However, they are not necessarily chatbots by initial design. Taking GPT models as our example, OpenAI specifically eschewed training on dialogue when creating its early GPT-2 model: "While dialog is an attractive approach, we worry it is overly restrictive. The internet contains a vast amount of information that is passively available without the need for interactive communication" (Radford 2019). Only in GPT's fourth iteration (ChatGPT/GPT-3.5) did OpenAI add dialog-style fine-tuning to create its now-familiar chatbot interface, which converted the prior GPT-3 model into an accessible format for widespread non-expert use. Earlier versions had been released to open-source tech communities — GPT-2 was released in piecemeal with dire warnings about the models' abilities and potentials, which is ironic given the continued expansion and extraction undertaken by OpenAI since (Radford 2019). OpenAI now releases proprietary tools, but much of the exponential expansion of its model from GPT-3 to 3.5 to 4-o, etc. has been in additional training meant to refine its function toward the chatbot interface — including fine-tuning using question answering datasets and reinforcement learning. It does not focus on building broad new domain knowledge, honing language skills, or applying new architectures (Brown 2020, OpenAI 2024, Ouyang 2022). The combination of a sufficiently large model with the chatbot interface has created a smooth, seemingly 'knowledgeable' interaction, echoing ELIZA’s earlier effect.

# **11.1 So, It Quacks Like a Duck, But How Does It Work?**

ELIZA demonstrated that seemingly sophisticated mechanisms can in fact be quite simple. Its structure reminds us that chatbots, like any computational systems, consist of logical operations and calculations — no matter how large or confounding they seem. The number of operations, the speed with which they are computed, and their compounding social effects together yield the same illusion that Weizenbaum spoke of when he sought a plausible script for ELIZA that would leave enough to the imagination to maintain the illusion for his conversational agent. Weizenbaum's words ring true today:  
"machines are made to behave in wondrous ways, often sufficient to dazzle even the most experienced observer. But once a particular program is unmasked, once its inner workings are explained in language sufficiently plain to induce understanding, its magic crumbles away; it stands revealed as a mere collection of procedures, each quite comprehensible. The observer says to himself 'I could have written that'." (Weizenbaum 1966)  
So-called intelligent systems—once their seemingly complex code base and basic concepts are laid bare — are no more intelligent or prescient than ELIZA was. Conversational interfaces can mask the scale and surprising simplicity of their operations. Today’s versions are larger and more intricate, but are still possible to be understood and critiqued, just like any system programmed in code. Critical code studies (Marino 2020a) reminds us that programmers have multiple approaches to understand any program, no matter how large, and that as readers of code we have opportunities to engage at many levels: from examining how code functions, to its programmers' stylistic or syntactic choices, to its material and historical contexts, to its cultural implications and influences.  
Let's look at specific processes that go into building and using LLMs and how they compare with ELIZA/DOCTOR's processes. Of course, the design and implementation of rule-based, programmatic systems like ELIZA are entirely different from node-based, probabilistic systems like LLMs. The former are often referred to as "deterministic," because the same choices, in the same order, will lead to the same outcomes, and the latter as "stochastic," because they operate by accumulations of probabilities. Yet both operate on the assumption that meaning can be operationalized—turned into what Weizenbaum called an "effective procedure"—which is to say, meaning can be defined through a set of fixed procedures. The processes themselves remain deterministic procedures, still driven by rule-based pattern matching, frequency scores, averages, and estimates—not human understanding. The suggestion of computational randomness in causal language models relies on this complexity and scale to mask the fact that even statistical decision-making is still procedural.[^2]  
By comparing techniques used to create LLMs and code from ELIZA/DOCTOR, we can compare what has changed in how language models produce facsimiles of meaning through code. I invite you to read these sections alongside our code annotations of ELIZA/DOCTOR (§7 and §8), "How ELIZA Works" (§6), and Weizenbaum's 1966 paper (Appendix) for full details on ELIZA/DOCTOR.

# **11.2 Transformer Models and Transformation Rules**

Each ELIZA script consists of a set of keyword rules that trigger “transformation” rules (see "How ELIZA Works" §6.1.5), and LLMs are designed using “transformer” models, yet these connote very different kinds of transformations.   
What is a transformer model? All machine learning models, including transformers, are the result of the model training process. This process depends on ingesting huge datasets and adjusting weighted variables based on those data (Ciston 2023). **Transformer models** are one architecture, or design style, for models that consist of steps that focus on a process called attention (discussed in depth below). Transformer models were designed originally for language translation, meant to convert existing information in one language into the same information in another language. Now they do all kinds of tasks: They generate multiple types of media, convert media to other media, answer questions, fill in blanks, detect and classify, and make GPTs (Generative Pretrained Transformers)—after a many-layered process of weighted probabilities and matrix multiplication.   
ELIZA's transformations had a similarly stunning effect with much simpler transformations in its day. Its rules for testing, decomposing, and recomposing original user prompts yielded plausible responses with rule-based transformations alone.

# **11.3 Datasets, Models, and Scripts (Oh, My\!)** 

To an extent, both ELIZA and LLMs are built to be adaptable, generalizable tools. In ELIZA each script is hand-programmed, customized for its specific task and domain. The approach offers interchangeability while considering specialized settings and contexts. This marks a distinction between generalizing software versus generalizing data.   
Today’s “zero-shot,” multitask, multimodal systems must collect as much information as possible by sweeping up vast swaths of online data. For predictive, generative models, the training dataset must be large enough to show the model millions of examples of text characters in order for it to develop a statistical model of which characters more often follow which other characters. They assume later customizations can be made with techniques like transfer learning that reuse (and amend) those data without knowledge of their original provenance or purpose.   
It is important to distinguish between the processes of **model training** and of **inference making**. Model training happens when the model is being created by its programmers. It results in a customized set of weights and rules to use when making inferences (Ciston 2023). The output is a set of files that are not human-readable—this is the model.   
For a system like ELIZA, an analogy would be a programmer writing a script like DOCTOR. That output is a programmed script stored on punch cards that the computer can run. In both cases, they determine in advance the kinds of interactions a user will go on to have. In LLMs, the model determines this through statistical prediction of the next characters (described in detail below). In ELIZA scripts, this is “modeled” manually in a more limited way (see §6). 

# **11.4 Inferences and Dialogues**

Meanwhile, **inference making** happens when a user writes a prompt and awaits an answer. In LLMs, this is when an existing model that has already been trained is now running. A user can write a prompt and the output is a generated text reply on screen. For ELIZA, this would be similar to a user interacting with an existing script. The output is a transcript printed from a teletype machine. These comparisons are metaphorical not literal, but meant to distinguish for both LLMs and ELIZA the parts of a program that happen during creation and programmer interaction (model training, script writing) versus the parts that happen during operation and user interaction (inference making, script running).

# **11.5 Tokenizers and TREAD**

In both ELIZA and LLMs, a user's input becomes a string of text that first needs to be **tokenized**. This process follows a series of predetermined rules to break up a text into words (or word fragments) called "tokens" so that each part can be used by a software program. For example, very simple tokenization might break a text string at each space, convert it to lowercase, put every word in its infinitive form, and remove all punctuation.   
Tokenization can be done automatically by software libraries like NLTK or SpaCy, and the boundaries of words will be largely determined by those libraries’ built-in tools. Will they keep proper nouns and contractions intact, for example? It depends on decisions made in advance. Depending on the tokenization technique, these tools can include a preset vocabulary list, language-specific standards for grammar, and special cases for domain-specific issues. Regardless of the methods chosen, tokenization greatly informs which concepts will be recognizable to an eventual model and how well any model will work (Liang n.d.) — not to mention for whom and for which purposes.  
Let's compare ELIZA's tokenization of a phrase with current LLM techniques. ELIZA takes prompt input from the user and puts it into a SLIP list using the function `TREAD` (ELIZA, line 250), by interpreting each space character as a delimiter. For example:

`['the_ _ _', 'stars_ _', 'are_ _ _', 'cloude', 'd_ _ _ _ _', 'over_ _', 'tonigh', 't_ _ _ _ _', '.']`

If a word is shorter than six characters, the SLIP list cell is filled out with blank characters. If the word contains more than six characters, it continues the word in a new SLIP list cell. These list items also contain links that identify them and position them in their lists, in order to retain the order of the phrase. This is described in more detail in §6.2.4.3.   
Compare this with contemporary tokenization in the GPT-3.5-turbo/GPT-4 tokenizer, which takes the same prompt and represent it as:

`['the', 'stars', 'are', 'cloud', 'ed', 'over', 'tonight' '.']`

After training, at inference time, GPT  also represents this sentence as:

`[791, 9958, 527, 9624, 291, 927, 18396, 13]`

Each number is a `token_id` for each word, based on its position in a vocabulary list that was created during training.[^3] We might imagine the `token_id` as not unlike ELIZA's `I=HASH.(WORD.5)` which creates a numerical representation for each word or subword from the prompt (ELIZA, line 282). It uses the sequence reader called `SCANER=SEQRDR.(KEY(I))` (ELIZA, line 282, 285\) to lookup that word in a list of keywords it already created and stored, specific to the script it is running. When it finds a match, it can proceed to the rules matching that keyword. If no match is found, it looks to the next word in the list it created from the user prompt. We can also imagine that keyword list `KEY` is a bit like a vocabulary list for an LLM, except the DOCTOR script's list is much, much smaller (“about 50 keywords” (Weizenbaum 1966, 38\) and composed by hand.[^4]  
Meanwhile, GPT-3 has a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens, built from an involved tokenization process during training that combines the 'rule-based' techniques described above with another set of 'character-based' tokenization processes: First, procedural 'rule-based' tokenization like that described above is performed on training datasets and only the most frequent words are retained in a list, as well as the number of times they appear. Next, 'character' tokenization is performed — not on the entire dataset, but on that vocabulary list. Called **byte-pair encoding**, this process begins with single characters (or bytes) and searches for the two characters that appear most frequently together. Repeating this, it looks for the next most frequent pairs of characters until it has sorted them all, then three-character strings, and so on, until common words in a dataset have been determined. In this case, the initial vocabulary list will be the set of characters (e.g. 256 bytes \+ `<|endoftext|>`), which will be paired together by frequency of co-occurrence until a new vocabulary list is created, its length determined by an arbitrary cutoff (e.g. 50,000 merges), optimized for "performance." The result is a text broken into computer-readable tokens, often not whole words but subwords. Byte-pair encoding was originally used for data compression and later applied to text (Sennrich 2015). The use of frequency as a means to sort and create tokens means that only the most common words in a training dataset will be retained in the model, while less frequently encountered words are "decomposed" to be represented as combinations of subwords, e.g. `["EL", "IZ", "A"]` becomes three tokens instead of one. Vocabulary and patterns of text that are less common to that dataset are less recognizable, less likely to resurface, and more likely to be lost.

# **11.6 Embedding and Precedence**

Turning words into numbers allows programs to perform calculations on words or parts of words, transforming them and comparing and transforming them just like any other kind of information. This is so basic it seems not worth mentioning, yet it is at the center of both the promise and problems of computation. We see this at the lowest to highest levels of abstraction in code, from machine and assembly languages to machine learning.   
In ELIZA, Weizenbaum added `precedence` scores to the keyword matching process. These scores act as rankings that are, in a sense, manually assigned weights for each keyword in any script. For example, if ELIZA/DOCTOR encounters a prompt like `["IF", "I", "REMEMBER" ...]`, it first applies the substitution rule `(I = YOU` to the user input (DOCTOR, line 188). Then, two keywords are encountered. In the DOCTOR script, `(REMEMBER 5 ...` has a precedence of 5 (DOCTOR, line 12), and `(IF 3 ...` has a precedence of 3 (line 28). Higher precedence weighs out, so ELIZA will prioritize the keyword `REMEMBER`, even though the keyword `IF` comes first in the phrase. Then the transformation rules from `((0 YOU REMEMBER )` will be applied, and ELIZA might return for example: `WHAT ELSE DO YOU REMEMBER` (DOCTOR line 12). ELIZA uses numerical precedence to weight the possible responses toward particular outcomes.   
In machine learning, **Embedding** is the process of creating a numerical representation of a token (word or subword), so that it can be used and compared with other tokens. It is output as a **vector**, a long set of often hundreds of decimal numbers. During model training, and again during inference, that long vector gets manipulated and reduced into a single number: a normalized likelihood that the token is a good fit for that particular context. If that likelihood is high, it is among the choices offered as the next word in a generative text phrase.  
There are several kinds of word embedding. **Token embedding** is context-independent. It describes tokens as they were established by model training — before they are used in a sentence or put in relation to any other tokens. This type of embedding is often used for comparing how similar two words are or used in search algorithms. The word "bank" will have a set token embedding (in a particular model). These can start out as random numbers or the values from training, which will eventually be processed further when put in context. These vectors come from comparing every vocabulary word against every other vocabulary word (with cross-wise matrix multiplication) and then condensing that comparison. Rather than a token embedding that is, say, 50,257 tokens long, separate machine learning tasks calculate the most "different" components to reduce the lengths of the token vectors substantially. In the GPT-3 and GPT-4 models, the vectors are 12,288 numbers long, and these are considered "dimensions" that will be compared once tokens are put in context.  
**Contextual embedding** describes each word or subword as it exists in a string of particular other words. Its vector values have been modified to represent the specific context of the words around it in this specific case. For our purposes, we will call words moving through a model "word vectors." This process changes the value in each position of their vectors (each "dimension"), by multiplying the vectors against other vectors as well as against the weights created during training.[^5] In a classic example of contextual embedding, the word vector for "bank" changes when it is either in the sentence, "I went to the river bank," or in "I need to get money from the bank." Word vectors will also change over time as they are being manipulated through the layers of a model. For example, Sevastjanova et al. used visualizations to compare the same word vector between different layers in the same model to see how they changed, finding that in some layers they were very similar and in others they could shift quite dramatically (Sevastjanova 2021).   
**Positional embedding** changes the word vector again to describe its particular place in a sentence. For example, the word "today" has a slightly different embedding in the sentence "Today might be sunny," than in, "It might be sunny today."   
In an LLM, such weights would be assigned statistically by training a model, rather than ELIZA's hard-coded, perhaps human-adjusted scores, but the effect works similarly. A transformer-based LLM will rank each word in a phrase as more or less important to each other word, based on its neighbors in general (context embedding) and its specific position in the sentence (positional encoding).

# **11.7 Self-Attention and Sequence Readers**

Context is essential to the “list” data type and its history. When working at General Electric, Weizenbaum et al. developed a precursor to SLIP called the Knotted List Structure (KLS) that focused on organizing data in relation to other data, not only to be recalled but also to be put in association (Weizenbaum et al. 1962). Those who were developing list structures at the time, including Weizenbaum, were creating some of the first relational data structures: These list data structures, like SLIP, stored not only the item itself and an address or index number (ID) for the item, but also an additional "link" to one or more other IDs — often pointing to its neighbors' addresses in the physical memory storage. SLIP also supported nested lists, allowing for complex data structures (Weizenbaum 1963).   
SLIP’s sequence reader `SEQRDR` lets ELIZA do keyword search, by reviewing a list of words in order, looking for words that match the `KEY` array. Sequence readers are SLIP lists that are used to traverse other lists. They can iterate a list and update their information about their position as they move, because of the relational nature of SLIP lists.   
Machine learning's use of word vectors for representation comes out of this history of array and list processing. The idea that a word or list item would exist in relation to its neighbors is fundamental to LLMs, in particular to self-attention and its contextual and positional embeddings.  
The process of attention, a key function in transformer-type models (Vaswani 2023), walks through every word vector in a sequence and examines many adjacent word vectors[^6] in order to modify that particular word vector in relation. This creates the contextual embedding discussed above that allows for a broader relationality in causal models. LLMs are programmed to compare many, or every, other word token's embedding (taken from the prior layer of the model) to each individual word token (self-attention), and they are programmed to perform many calculations in parallel (multi-head attention). They compare these through scaled dot product matrix multiplication (basic multiplication with each other and with the weights), using huge matrices which are the dimensions of the word vectors themselves. By repeating this process, layer by layer, the word tokens for every subword in the model are continually adjusted and finally normed to a number between 0 and 1 to represent the likelihood that they will be the next expected word or subword.  
"An attention function can be described as mapping a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output, where the query, keys, values, and output are all vectors. \[...\] In a self-attention layer all of the keys, values and queries come from the same place, in this case, the output of the previous layer in the encoder. Similarly, self-attention layers in the decoder allow each position in the decoder to attend to all positions in the decoder up to and including that position." (Vaswani 2023\)  
In this function, the query is the word token that is the focus of the self-attention function at that moment. The query searches through all of the other dimensions of the model, by multiplying its dimensions by the dimensions of the keys (labels). The result is a score representing the likelihood that this word vector (the value in this key-value pair) fits the query. Next all scores get multiplied against their respective word vectors (values) (Alammar n.d.). This process is performed on every pertinent token in the phrase (often in parallel) until each token has been analyzed in relation to the others.  
This process is the crux of transformer models that "understand" words in their context. These attention layers are sandwiched by feed forward network layers, which apply algebraic functions to find and emphasize more patterns in the data. At the end of each layer, additional functions adjust the results so they are ready to be interpreted by the next layer. Finally, each datapoint gets shifted into a range between 0 and 1, reducing it to a probability (a simple function called `softmax`). These probabilities represent the likelihood for each token in the model's vocabulary list that it could be the next token in the output phrase.

# **11.8 Pseudorandomness and a Certain Counting Mechanism**

Of course, the output phrase has not been written yet, so there is much that can be done to intervene\! In machine learning tasks, settings called "hyperparameters" are variables that we can adjust to change how the model runs on a prompt and to modify its potential outputs. Well-tuned hyperparameters help LLMs appear both 'knowledgeable' and 'natural' through their calibration of pseudorandomness through sampling methods. Here are some common hyperparameters:  
`temperature` is a factor that is applied to the raw outputs before they are normalized that adjusts how much impact they have. It acts as a kind of threshold that affects how likely they are to be passed on to the next layer as meaningful or valuable (if at all). A temperature less than 1 appears to reduce 'randomness', because when the raw output is divided by the temperature it increases its value, strengthening the already strong scores. A temperature higher than 1 appears to increase 'randomness', because when the raw output is divided by the temperature it decreases its value, flattening the difference between scores to some degree. `frequency_penalty` and `presence_penalty` act on pre-normalized data to dissuade the model from choosing tokens that have been used already, either increasing with frequency or appearing at all, respectively.  
After norming, the word vectors are reduced to and sorted by their probability scores. Rather than outputting a single result, LLMs usually offer a list of options from which to sample. Setting different hyperparameters that intervene post-norming adjusts how much variability (or pseudorandomness) is available in that selection process.  
The `top_k` hyperparameter determines how many tokens are available to sample. The tokens are sorted by score, and these scores become probabilities that a given token will be selected. Tokens higher on the list are more likely, but not guaranteed to be chosen. For example, `top_k = 30` means it will select from the top 30 most likely words based on its resulting inference, and a higher `top_k` value makes for a wilder output potential. Set `top_p` to a total likelihood instead of a number of tokens to be sampled. The hyperparameter will dynamically adjust the length of the list to include enough tokens that they sum up to that total likelihood. Other options like `min_p` and `top_a` are variations and combinations of these, which try to optimize for different tasks with differing goals. And rather than a "greedy" search, `beam_size` and `top_beams` adjust for the number of hypothetical options to search for and return (Alammar n.d., Tunstall et al 2022).  
No such hyperparameters exist in ELIZA, but checkpoints are in place to trigger enough seeming spontaneity in order to maintain that same illusion of 'knowledge' and 'naturalness'. The most remarkable — referred to as "a certain counting mechanism" (Weizenbaum 1966\) — combines a counter and storage to lend extra mystique to the bot. Along with its keyword-based transformations, a special keyword is designated that will save its user inputs into a memory list. In DOCTOR, the keyword `MY` is the memory keyword, so any time a user’s prompt activates the keyword `MY`, along with the regular response produced, their input is also is saved to `MEMORY` after being transformed with one of these rules (based on the encoded (hashed) version of the last word of the user prompt)(ELIZA, lines 315-331, §6.2):

`67  (MEMORY MY`  
`68  	(0 YOUR 0 = LETS DISCUSS FURTHER WHY YOUR 3)`  
`69  	(0 YOUR 0 = EARLIER YOU SAID YOUR 3)`  
`70  	(0 YOUR 0 = BUT YOUR 3)`  
`71  	(0 YOUR 0 = DOES THAT HAVE ANYTHING TO DO WITH THE FACT THAT YOUR 3))`

A counter called `LIMIT` with a range 1-4 keeps track of `MEMORY`'s storage state. Later when ELIZA encounters a user prompt without any keyword matches, and when `LIMIT = 4`, it will return the first-stored entry from its memory list (removing it from its list to avoid reuse). This offers the human-computer interaction a sense of retained knowledge that feels like 'listening', especially in the context of a trained therapist persona.  
ELIZA is designed to 'decompose and recompose' text by applying transformation rules with an element of added sampling intervening (Weizenbaum 1966); while LLMs 'encode and decode' text by combining programmatic and probabilistic approaches, with variables available to intervene in its sampling.

# **11.9 Guardrails and CHANGE**

Model training rarely stops at the first inference; models are continually updated after they are created — either in order to refine them toward their original objectives or to 'fine-tune' them toward new goals — by adding new training data on top of them. Weizenbaum also saw the potential for ELIZA scripts to be adapted as their programmers learned how their users interacted with them, which makes sense given that the original interest in ELIZA was to study human-computer interactions: "An important consequence of the editing facility built into ELIZA is that a given ELIZA script need not start out to be a large, full-blown scenario. On the contrary, it should begin as a quite modest set of keywords and transformation rules and permitted to be grown and molded as experience with it builds" (Weizenbaum 1966). As part of this, ELIZA has built-in functions for being updated on the fly. The `CHANGE` function (ELIZA, line 258\) lets one edit script rules by starting input with a `+` character. In some versions of ELIZA, starting a prompt with the `*` character would allow a user to create a new list with a new transformation rule, which they could insert into the current script (ELIZA, line 261). Weizenbaum could modify ELIZA scripts as he saw how people interacted with it. This dynamism makes ELIZA a (very nascent) ancestor of today's causal models, understood to be continually adaptable to additional training data, which use a variety of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and other forms of fine-tuning.   
Every program needs error handling, and language models are no `Exception`. In ELIZA, when a keyword is found in a user's input text, it's business as usual. Different rules are chosen and prioritized, based on the kind of keyword it finds. However, ELIZA addresses when user prompts fall outside of expected areas by requiring scripts to have a `NONE` rule (ELIZA, lines 235-237) that will handle situations when no keyword is found or pattern matching has failed. Getting generative AI to do what you want may seem as simple as asking, yet a whole deterministic infrastructure of guardrails help LLMs maintain what the industry calls "alignment": getting systems to do what is expected, desired, and safe, while preventing them from performing activities outside those boundaries (anything that would crash the system, create liability, etc.). Safety and alignment can be entire layers of work on top of the initial training of an LLM, and they require continual effort while users experiment with what models can do. Models are not retrained overnight. Problems are manually patched, until the next big release can incorporate a solution that includes variations on that example. Even so, the process used to create model releases that include better AI safety also relies on human decisions and direct intervention by programmers and data workers.  
In order to maintain guardrails and moderate content, LLM makers combine their probabilistic techniques with the programmatic techniques seen in the era of ELIZA: rules, taxonomies, keywords, and lexicons. The latest OpenAI models maintain alignment using reinforcement learning techniques supported by both humans and other ML models, yet both types rely on people labeling content, creating rules and rubrics, and writing content policies (OpenAI et al. 2024). OpenAI's "Model Spec," like a meta-script, describes the rules for all its models — "Follow the chain of command, Comply with applicable laws, Don't provide information hazards, Respect creators and their rights, Protect people's privacy, Don't respond with NSFW (not safe for work) content" — and gives default statements applied in different gatekeeping circumstances (OpenAI “Model Spec” n.d.). Not unlike ELIZA's different tiers of error handling, models then get trained to provide "hard refusals" or "soft refusals" depending on the ranking of the request's severity (Mu et al. 2024).

# **Conclusion**

## *ELIZA Raises Questions, Cautions, & Potentials*

Not all AI systems are machine learning, and not all generative AI are transformer-based language models. But the next generation of multimodal models remain text-reliant at their core, requiring huge datasets of labels, captions, and prompts. Each cutting-edge technology retains the assumptions laid in its foundations by generations of predecessors. And these emerging, powerful tools are the catalyst of a host of conversations and concerns around the future of computation. Each computational hype cycle becomes a synecdoche for innovation itself — leaving no room for other solutions, let alone other goals, other ways of knowing, or other imaginaries.  
ELIZA emerged in a similar moment at the juncture of many computational innovations — ranging widely from string processing to speech processing to statistical modeling. Weizenbaum began warning about the growing impacts of these advancements, from his particular perspective as a technologist and a Jewish German-American post-WWII witnessing the uses of technology in Vietnam. Many of the questions he asked are still being posed today, and his works offer reflections and reminders for the future.

## *Look for What's Missing*

Our desire for connection is so strong that we underestimate what we contribute when we relate with machines. Despite being innovative in so many ways, ELIZA shows how little complexity is required to create relatable human-computer interaction. People are willing to go to great lengths to fill in information gaps, in order to make sense of a story. Often gaps are intentionally included from the start, as with the persona of the Rogerian therapist that was chosen for its plausible (even wise) unknowing. Even as little as a pseudorandomized 'yes' or 'no' response becomes enough to feel personal exchange, given the right framing Garfinkel 2019\).[^7] Weizenbaum says, “The human speaker will contribute much to clothe ELIZA's responses in vestments of plausibility” (1967). With his reminder, we can learn to notice what is missing in all our interactions with automated systems — in the limits of the language exchanged, in the systems hidden behind interfaces.    
Don't assume complexity or dynamic growth behind an interface. Just because a chatbot interacts well, or gets some information correct, does not mean its other outputs are accurate or trustworthy. To quote Mr. Mind, “I AM A BOT ALL BOTS ARE LIARS.” Don't look for intelligence or feeling behind those chatty expressions. The bot is not your friend. (Nor is it your enemy. It's just logic and math, a *lot* of it moving very fast, putting one word after another.)  
Do look for what aspects of current chatbots are missing. We can ask more often: What aren't they telling or showing? What isn't being offered? Where are we doing most of the work of creating connection? We can also ask: Who is left out of the stories? Who is left out of the room when they are being created? Who is included without their consent? Whose story is represented one-dimensionally?

## *Remap Understanding*

Weizenbaum also insisted on distinguishing between how models are designed often to "just work" and how models necessarily map a theory of the world (1972). These objectives often blur, with the results reflecting back to culture a computational representation of the world in which only its most "machine-readable" parts are validated. Weizenbaum argues,   
"To those fully in the grip of the computer metaphor, to understand X is to be able to write a computer program that realizes X. \[...\] In other words, the computer metaphor has become another lamppost under whose light, and only under whose light, men seek answers to burning questions." (1976, 157–158)   
In settling for programs that "just work," we risk assuming that because a program works it can also provide an adequate or accurate depiction of the world, or the problem at hand. Sometimes this is adequate to that particular experiment or use case, but can also get carried out of context or baked into the next foundation model. For example, models and derivatives continue to be built from the popular datasets like ImageNet (2009)—despite the fact that it has been critiqued and amended for containing over 600,000 “potentially offensive” images, and despite its own basis on the outdated WordNet taxonomy (1990) (Corry 2021). Foundation models’ popularity leads to standardized training, thus standardized patterns and errors: “models trained on ImageNet often rely on the same ‘spurious cues’ and ‘shortcuts’, for example using background textures like green grass to predict foreground object classes such as cows (Bommasani 2022). \[This is how we end up with language models that generate retro, reductive versions of the world, because that is the world that they have been trained on.\]

If "programming is theory building," as Peter Naur (1985) suggests, the theory of the world that ELIZA maps is one in which understanding is always partial. In writing the ELIZA program, Weizenbaum theorized a form of conversation that can be exchanged through a set of predetermined templates, rules, and syntax swaps — without the need for understanding. In the DOCTOR script, a therapist persona is an incomplete reflection and a transformative echo. Weizenbaum mapped a theory of communication built on misunderstanding itself, and he was interested in what could be made productive from the \[slippages/errors/mistakes\] between people and machines (Weizenbaum n.d.). He continually returned focus to questions of (the impossibility of) human understanding rather than computational understanding: "the very asking of the question, 'Has the computer captured the essence of man?' is a diversion and, in that sense, a trap. For the real question, 'Does man understand the essence of man?' cannot be answered by any technological instrument" (1972, 611). Don't assume computer 'understanding' is happening, or that it is a useful or achievable goal. Even during person-to-person communication, we continue to converse productively despite knowing we will never arrive at full mutual understanding.  
Lack of machine understanding is not a barrier to human-computer communication, and neither should machine understanding be our aim. Perhaps we can hold computational agents to a different standard. Rather than task them with unquantifiable goals like intelligence, might we embrace Weizenbaum's insistence that bots do not know. Instead, we can expect from them and their makers only partial theories — knowing as we do their structures and their limitations, and our own. Rather than trying to optimize for understanding, we can reach for something partial, something lighter. We can interact with automated systems and not expect their agency, but instead use them to learn more about what we don't know. As Weizenbaum says, "Indeed, one of the most cogent reasons for using computers is to expose holes in our thinking. Computers are merciless critics in this respect" (1976, 65). Might we imagine computational perspectives that are entirely different from our perspective, rather than trying to mimic what we already think we know?  
What else can computers do besides mimic concepts defined by people. Instead of who is better at art, humans or machines, or who understands better or faster, ask: What kind of things can machines do well that we cannot? Such questions could help us expand our ideas of what machines \*can\* do and what they are for, help us make space and generosity for the non-human. They can help us make space and generosity for the humanity we ignore. Rather than can a machine understand, Weizenbaum was more interested in the question: Can a person understand? Can we use the tools of computation to help a person understand? To build a relationship with other people and with the non-human world around us.

## *Relate Differently*

Weizenbaum resisted anthropomorphizing computer systems and warned about their illusory charms. In building ELIZA to be adaptable to other scripts, he also mapped the potential for software tools not beholden to any single imaginary. In the SPACKS script, ELIZA discusses the poem "The Freshmen" by Barry Spacks. The resulting dialogue unpacks the meaning of a line referencing a WWII German concentration camp: "Is any heart in order after Belsen?" Consider if ELIZA were not known solely as a feminized therapy bot, but as a "translating processor," or as a math teacher, or a poet, or as a multifaceted tool that was all of these. If ELIZA had been known by its other scripts, imagine its legacies in those alternate futures. What alternate scripts and alternate futures can we imagine for AI systems now? What would you wish a bot to do if someone had not already told you what it should do? Imagine what causal models, and computation in general, could offer if we were not fixed into the models we have been given that focus on imitating intelligence[^8] in one particular optimized, machine-readable representation. ELIZA offers new potential scripts for human-computer relationality, beyond a reductive focus on mimicry, analogy, parody, categorization, and stereotype by default.  
Causal language models remain less predictable than rule-based systems like ELIZA, but their unpredictability does not mean they are uninterpretable or unimpeachable. In their scale and complexity, it is easy to forget that they require just as much human decision-making, influence, and control. Weizenbaum insists that we can "deny technology the prerogative to formulate man's questions" (1972). Computer systems are not neutral. We build them and give them direction. When we say, "Please, go on..." we must also ask, "Where do we want to go? Where do we want to avoid?" Those questions have consequences for us all.

# So, It Quacks Like a Duck, But How Does It Work?

ELIZA proves that seemingly sophisticated mechanisms can in fact be quite simple. Its structure reminds us that chatbots, like any computational systems, consist of logical operations and calculations — no matter how large or confounding they seem. The number of operations, the speed with which they are computed, and their compounding social effects together yield the same illusion that Weizenbaum spoke of when he sought a plausible script for ELIZA that would leave enough to the imagination to maintain the illusion for his conversational agent. Weizenbaum's words ring true today:

>"machines are made to behave in wondrous ways, often sufficient to dazzle even the most experienced observer. But once a particular program is unmasked, once its inner workings are explained in language sufficiently plain to induce understanding, its magic crumbles away; it stands revealed as a mere collection of procedures, each quite comprehensible. The observer says to himself 'I could have written that'." [@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966]

So-called intelligent systems — once their seemingly complex code base and basic concepts are laid bare — are no more intelligent or prescient than ELIZA was. Conversational interfaces can even help mask the scale and surprising simplicity of their operations. Today’s versions are larger and more intricate, but are still possible to be understood and critiqued, just like any system programmed in code. Critical code studies [@marinoCriticalCodeStudies2020a] reminds us that programmers have multiple approaches to understand any program, no matter how large, and that as readers of code we have opportunities to engage at many levels: from examining how code functions, to its programmers' stylistic or syntactic choices, to its material and historical contexts, to its cultural implications and influences.

Let's look at specific processes that go into building and using LLMs and how they compare with ELIZA/DOCTOR's processes. Of course, the design and implementation of rule-based, programmatic systems like ELIZA are entirely different from node-based, probabilistic systems like LLMs. The former are often referred to as "deterministic," because the same choices will lead to the same outcomes, and the latter as "stochastic," because they operate by accumulations of probabilities. Yet both operate on the assumption that meaning can be operationalized — turned into what Weizenbaum called an "effective procedure" — which is to say, meaning can be defined through a set of fixed procedures. The processes themselves remain basic mathematic procedures, still driven by rule-based pattern matching, frequency scores, averages, and estimates — not human-like understanding. The suggestion of computational randomness in causal language models relies on this complexity and scale to mask the fact that even statistical decision-making is still procedural.

By comparing techniques used to create LLMs and code from ELIZA/DOCTOR, we can compare what has changed in how language models produce facsimiles of meaning through code. I invite you to read these sections alongside our code annotations of ELIZA/DOCTOR (Chapters [XX]), "How ELIZA Works" (Chapter [XX]), and Weizenbaum's 1966 paper (Chapter [XX]) for full details on ELIZA/DOCTOR.

## Datasets, Models, and Inferences; Scripts and Transcripts

Let's first distinguish between **model training** and **inference making**. Model training happens when the model is being created by its programmers. It processes a collection of datasets through a predetermined structure, in order to create a customized set of rules it can refer back to when making inferences [@cistonCRITICALFIELDGUIDE2023]. The output is a set of files that are not human-readable. For a system like ELIZA, an analogy would be a programmer writing a script like DOCTOR. The output is a programmed script stored on punch cards that the computer can reproduce. 

Inference making happens when a user writes a prompt and awaits an answer. In LLMs, this is when an existing model that has already been trained is now running. A user can write a prompt and the output is a generated text reply on screen. For ELIZA, this would be similar to a user interacting with an existing script. The output is a transcript printed from a teletype machine. This comparison is metaphorical not literal, but meant to distinguish for both LLMs and ELIZA the parts of a program that happen during creation and programmer interaction (model training, script writing) versus the parts that happen during operation and user interaction (inference making, script running).

Weizenbaum imagined ELIZA as a generalizable tool, not as a therapy bot. He built ELIZA to process different scripts to be used for different tasks. The DOCTOR script is the best known, and became synonymous with ELIZA; however, each script was customized and hand-programmed to its particular task. Weizenbaum collaborated with psychologists for the DOCTOR script, mathematicians for the math scripts, and with a poet for the poetry script. Both ELIZA and LLMs offer adaptable tools; but ELIZA demonstrates domain-specific interchangeability and considers specialized setting and context. This marks a distinction between generalizing software versus generalizing data. Unlike ELIZA's custom scripts created for specific domains, today's automated systems collect as much information as possible by sweeping up vast swaths of online data. They create giant “zero-shot” multi-tool models, upon which later customizations can be made, with techniques like transfer learning that reuse those data without knowledge of their original provenance or purpose. 

## Transformers and Transformation Rules

Each ELIZA script has a set of keyword rules that trigger transformation rules (see "How ELIZA Works" §5.1.5), and LLMs use Transformer model designs, yet these connote very different kinds of transformations. Transformer models were designed originally for language translation, meant to convert existing information in one language into the same information in another language. Now they do all kinds of tasks: They generate multiple types of media, convert media to other media, answer questions, fill in blanks, detect and classify, and of course generate text, as they do in the GPT (Generative Pretrained Transformers) models — after a many-layered process of passing values through a complicated set of calculations, mostly involving enormous matrix multiplication. ELIZA's transformations had a similarly stunning effect with much simpler transformations in its day. Its rules for testing, decomposing, and recomposing the original text yield plausible responses with rule-based transformations alone. 

## Tokenizers and TREAD 

In both ELIZA and LLMs, a user's input becomes a string of text that first needs to be **tokenized**. This process follows a series of predetermined rules to break up a text into words (or word fragments) called "tokens" so that each part can be used by a software program. For example, very simple tokenization might break a text string at each space, convert it to lowercase, put every word in its infinitive form, and remove all punctuation. 

Tokenization can be done automatically by software libraries like NLTK or SpaCy, and the boundaries of words will be largely determined by those libraries’ built-in tools. Will they keep proper nouns and contractions intact, for example? It depends on decisions made in advance. Depending on the tokenization technique, these tools can include a preset vocabulary list, language-specific standards for grammar, and special cases for domain-specific issues. Regardless of the methods chosen, tokenization greatly informs which concepts will be recognizable to an eventual model and how well any model will work [@liangStanfordCS324] — not to mention for whom and for which purposes.

Let's compare ELIZA's tokenization of a phrase with current LLM techniques. ELIZA takes prompt input from the user and puts it into a SLIP list using the function `TREAD` (ELIZA, line 250), by interpreting each space character as a delimiter. For example:

`['the_ _ _', 'stars_ _', 'are_ _ _', 'cloude', 'd_ _ _ _ _', 'over_ _', 'tonigh', 't_ _ _ _ _', '.']`

If a word is shorter than six characters, the SLIP list cell is filled out with blank characters. If the word contains more than six characters, it continues the word in a new SLIP list cell. These list items also contain links that identify them and position them in their lists, in order to retain the order of the phrase. This is described in more detail in §5.2.4.3. 

Compare this with contemporary tokenization in the GPT-3.5-turbo/GPT-4 tokenizer, which takes the same prompt and represent it as:

```['the', 'stars', 'are', 'cloud', 'ed', 'over', 'tonight' '.']```

It also represents this sentence as:

```[791, 9958, 527, 9624, 291, 927, 18396, 13]``` 

Those numbers are the `token_id`s for each word, based on its position in a vocabulary list created during training.[^inference-time] We could imagine the `token_id` as not unlike ELIZA's `I=HASH.(WORD.5)` which creates a numerical representation for each word or subword (ELIZA, line 282). It uses the function  `SCANER=SEQRDR.(KEY(I))` (ELIZA, line 282) to lookup that word in a list of keywords it already created and stored, specific to the script it is running. When it finds a match, it can proceed to the rules matching that keyword. If no match is found, it looks to the next word in the list it created from the user prompt. We could also imagine that keyword list `KEY(I)` as a bit like a vocabulary list for an LLM, except the DOCTOR script's list is much, much smaller ([XXX-COUNT?]) and composed by hand.[^seqrdr]

[^seqrdr]: For more on the `SEQRDR` function used for navigating those lists, see "Self-Attention and Sequence Reader" below.

[^inference-time]: At model inference time, the process looks much more simple at this stage. It finds the `token_id` for each token in a vocabulary lookup table that was created during training, then uses the `token_id` to look up the longer embedding information about each token, which was also determined during training. It also retains information about the token's position in the sequence. This is discussed more below in "Self-Attention and Sequence Reader."

Meanwhile, GPT-3 has a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens, built from an involved tokenization process during training that combines the 'rule-based' techniques described above with another set of 'character-based' tokenization processes: First, procedural 'rule-based' tokenization like that described above is performed on training datasets and only the most frequent words are retained in a list, as well as the number of times they appear. Next, 'character' tokenization is performed - not on the entire dataset, but on that vocabulary list. Called **byte-pair encoding**, this process begins with single characters (or bytes) and searches the two characters that appear most frequently together. Repeating this, it looks for the next most frequent pairs of characters until it has sorted them all, then three-character strings, and so on, until common words in a dataset have been determined. In this case, the initial vocabulary list will be the set of characters (e.g. 256 bytes + `<|endoftext|>`), which will be paired together by frequency of co-occurrence until a new vocabulary list is created, its length determined by an arbitrary cutoff (e.g. 50,000 merges), optimized for "performance." The result is a text broken into computer-readable tokens, often not whole words but subwords [@SummaryTokenizers]. Byte-pair encoding was originally used for data compression and later applied to text [@sennrichNeuralMachineTranslation2015]. The use of frequency as a means to sort and create tokens means that only the most common words in a training dataset will be retained in the model, while less frequently encountered words are "decomposed" to be represented as combinations of subwords, e.g. `["EL", "IZ", "A"]` becomes three tokens instead of one. Vocabulary and patterns of text that are less common to that dataset are less recognizable, less likely to resurface, and more likely to be lost. 

## Embedding and Precedence

Turning words into numbers allows programs to perform calculations on words or parts of words, transforming them and comparing and transforming them just like any other kind of information. This is so basic it seems not worth mentioning, yet it is at the center of both the promise and problems of computation. We see this at the lowest to highest levels of abstraction in code, from machine and assembly languages to machine learning. 

In ELIZA, Weizenbaum added `precedence` scores to the keyword matching process. These scores act as rankings that are, in a sense, manually assigned weights for each keyword in any script. For example, if ELIZA/DOCTOR encounters a prompt like `["IF", "I", "REMEMBER" ...]`, it first applies the substitution rule `(I = YOU` to the user input (DOCTOR, line 188). Then, two keywords are encountered. In the DOCTOR script, `(REMEMBER 5 ...` has a precedence of 5 (DOCTOR, line 12), and `(IF 3 ...` has a precedence of 3 (line 28). Higher precedence weighs out, so ELIZA will prioritize the keyword `REMEMBER`, even though the keyword `IF` comes first in the phrase. Then the transformation rules from `((0 YOU REMEMBER )` will be applied, and ELIZA might return for example: `WHAT ELSE DO YOU REMEMBER` (DOCTOR line 12). ELIZA uses numerical precedence to weight the possible responses toward particular outcomes. 

In machine learning, **Embedding** is the process of creating a numerical representation of a token (word or subword), so that it can be used and compared with other tokens. It is output as a **vector**, a long set of often hundreds of decimal numbers. During model training, and again during inference, that long vector gets manipulated and reduced into a single number: a normalized likelihood that the token is a good fit for that particular context. If that likelihood is high, it is among the choices offered as the next word in a generative text phrase.

There are several kinds of word embedding. **Token embedding** is context-independent. It describes tokens as they were established by model training — before they are used in a sentence or put in relation to any other tokens. This type of embedding is often used for comparing how similar two words are or used in search algorithms. The word "bank" will have a set token embedding (in a particular model). These can start out as random numbers or the values from training, which will eventually be processed further when put in context. These vectors come from comparing every vocabulary word against every other vocabulary word (with cross-wise matrix multiplication) and then condensing that comparison. Rather than a token embedding that is, say, 50,257 tokens long, separate machine learning tasks calculate the most "different" components to reduce the lengths of the token vectors substantially. In the GPT-3 and GPT-4 models, the vectors are 12,288 numbers long, and these are considered "dimensions" that will be compared once tokens are put in context.

**Contextual embedding**, describes each word or subword as it exists in a string of particular other words. Its vector values have been modified to represent the specific context of the words around it in this specific case. For our purposes, we will call words moving through a model "word vectors." This process changes the value in each position of their vectors (each "dimension"), by multiplying the vectors against other vectors as well as against the weights created during training. In a classic example of contextual embedding, the word vector for "bank" changes when it is either in the sentence, "I went to the river bank," or in "I need to get money from the bank." Word vectors will also change over time as they are being manipulated through the layers of a model. For example, Sevastjanova et al. used visualizations to compare the same word vector between different layers in the same model to see how they changed, finding that in some layers they were very similar and in others they could shift quite dramatically  [@sevastjanovaExplainingContextualizationLanguage2021]. 

Finally, **positional embedding** will change the word vector again to describe its particular place in a sentence. For example, the word "today" will have a slightly different embedding in the sentence "Today might be sunny," than in, "It might be sunny today." 

In an LLM, such weights would be assigned statistically by training a model, rather than ELIZA's hard-coded, perhaps human-adjusted scores, but the effect works similarly. A transformer-based LLM will rank each word in a phrase as more or less important to each other word, based on its neighbors in general (context embedding) and its specific position in the sentence (positional encoding).

[^weight-training]: Weights are multipliers that affect data moving through each layer (stage) of the model. As such, they encourage or discourage particular patterns — multiply a datapoint by 5 and it will move to the next layer with more influence, multiply it by 0.1 and it will have almost no influence on the next layer. After beginning with random weights, the model training process establishes and finalizes what those weights should be, based on a desired outcome determined by programmers. Then, during user prompting and model inference, the weights remain the fixed numbers that were set as a result of model training.

## Self-Attention and Sequence Readers

Common to both ELIZA and LLMs is the technique of string iteration using lists: An LLM converts words into a vector (similar to an array, or list of numbers) then uses that to calculate the probability that words appear near each other. ELIZA reviews a list of words in order, looking for keywords that match, using what SLIP calls a sequence reader: Sequence readers are lists that are used to traverse other lists. S=SEQRDR.(INPUT) (ELIZA, line 262) initializes the variable S as a sequence reader to iterate over the words in the list INPUT. They are iterators that store memory of their position, and they can descend into a list and can also report back their position with LPNTR() ("list pointer"), also updating their position as they move. The sequence reader allows for keyword search, which facilitates the transformations and subsequently contextual responses ELIZA provides. 

Context is essential at a fundamental level of the data type, too. When working at General Electric, Weizenbaum et al. developed a precursor to SLIP called the Knotted List Structure (KLS) that focused on organizing data in relation to other data, not only to be recalled but also to be put in association [@weizenbaumKnottedListStructures1962]. Those developing list structures at the time, including Weizenbaum, were creating some of the first relational data structures: These list data structures, like SLIP, stored the item itself, an address or index number (ID) of the item that other items could reference, plus an additional "link" to one or more other IDs — often pointing to its neighbors' addresses in the physical memory storage. SLIP supported sublists could be nested within each other, allowing for complex data structures [@weizenbaumSymmetricListProcessor1963]. Machine learning's use of word vectors for representation comes out of this history of array and list processing. The idea that a word or list item would exist in relation to its neighbors is fundamental to LLMs, in particular to self-attention and its contextual and positional embeddings. 

The process of attention, a key function in transformer-type models [@vaswaniAttentionAllYou2023], walks through every word vector in a sequence and examines many adjacent word vectors[^n-gram] in order to modify that particular word vector in relation. This creates the contextual embedding discussed above that allows for a broader relationality in causal models. LLMs are programmed to compare many, or every, other word token's embedding (taken from the prior layer of the model) to each individual word token (self-attention), and they are programmed to perform many calculations in parallel (multi-head attention). They compare these through scaled dot product matrix multiplication (basic multiplication with each other and with the weights), using huge matrices which are the dimensions of the word vectors themselves. By repeating this process, layer by layer, the word tokens for every subword in the model are continually adjusted and finally normed to a number between 0 and 1 to represent the likelihood that they will be the next expected word or subword.

[^n-gram]: A bit like n-grams on steroids, if you will. For example, GPT-3 has a context window of 2,048 tokens [@brownLanguageModelsAre2020], meaning for each token it examines that many adjacent tokens. 

>"An attention function can be described as mapping a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output, where the query, keys, values, and output are all vectors. [...] In a self-attention layer all of the keys, values and queries come from the same place, in this case, the output of the previous layer in the encoder. Similarly, self-attention layers in the decoder allow each position in the decoder to attend to all positions in the decoder up to and including that position" [@vaswaniAttentionAllYou2023].

In this function, the query is the word token that is the focus of the self-attention function at that moment. The query searches through all of the other dimensions of the model, by multiplying its dimensions by the dimensions of the keys (labels). The result is a score representing the likelihood that this word vector (the value in this key-value pair) fits the query. Next all scores get multiplied against their respective word vectors (values) [@alammarIllustratedGPT2Visualizing]. This process is performed on every pertinent token in the sequence (often in parallel) until each token has been analyzed in relation to the others. 

This process is the crux of transformer models that "understand" words in their context. These attention layers are sandwiched by feed forward network layers, which apply algebraic functions to find and emphasize more patterns in the data. At the end of each layer, additional functions adjust the results so they are ready to be interpreted by the next layer. Finally, each datapoint gets shifted into a range between 0 and 1, reducing it to a probability (a simple function called `softmax`). These probabilities represent the likelihood for each token in the model's vocabulary list that it could be the next token in the output sequence. 

## Pseudorandomness and a Certain Counting Mechanism

Of course, this sequence has not been written yet, so there is much that can be done to intervene! In machine learning tasks, different settings called "hyperparameters" are the variables that we can adjust to change how the model runs on our prompt and to modify its potential outputs. These hyperparameters affect the "decoding" process and help LLMs appear both 'knowledgeable' and 'naturalness' through their adjustment of pseudorandomness through sampling methods. Let's look at some common hyperparameters:

`temperature` is a factor that is applied to the raw outputs before they are normalized that adjusts how much impact they have. It acts as a kind of threshold. This affects whether they are passed on at all — that is, how likely they are to be passed on to the next layer as meaningful or valuable. 
A temperature less than 1 appears to reduce 'randomness', because when the raw output is divided by the temperature it increases its value, strengthening the already strong scores. A temperature higher than 1 appears to increase 'randomness', because when the raw output is divided by the temperature it decreases its value, flattening the difference between scores to some degree. `frequency_penalty` and `presence_penalty` act on pre-normalized data to dissuade the model from choosing tokens that have been used already, either increasing with frequency or appearing at all, respectively.

After norming, the word vectors are reduced to and sorted by their probability scores. Rather than outputting a single result, LLMs usually offer a list of options from which to sample. Setting different hyperparameters that intervene post-norming adjusts how much variability (or pseudorandomness) is available in that selection process. 

The `top_k` hyperparameter determines how many tokens are available to sample. The tokens are sorted by score, and these scores become probabilities that a given token will be selected. Tokens higher on the list are more likely, but not guaranteed to be chosen. For example, `top_k = 30` means it will select from the top 30 most likely words based on its resulting inference, and a higher `top_k` value makes for a wilder output potential. Set `top_p` to a total likelihood instead of a number of tokens to be sampled. The hyperparameter will dynamically adjust the length of the list to include enough tokens that they sum up to that total likelihood. Other options like `min_p` and `top_a` are variations and combinations of these, which try to optimize for different tasks with differing goals. And rather than a "greedy" search, `beam_size` and `top_beams` adjust for the number of hypothetical options to search for and return [@alammarIllustratedGPT2Visualizing; @alammarIllustratedTransformer; @NaturalLanguageProcessing]. 

No such hyperparameters exist in ELIZA, but several checkpoints are in place to trigger enough seeming spontaneity in order to maintain that same illusion of 'knowledge' and 'naturalness'. The most remarkable — referred to as "a certain counting mechanism" [@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966] — combines a counter and storage to lend extra mystique to the bot. Along with its keyword-based transformations, a special keyword is designated that will save its user inputs into a memory list. In DOCTOR, the keyword `MY` is the memory keyword, so every time the user prompts with a sequence containing `MY`, it is saved to `MEMORY` after being transformed with one of these rules (based on the encoded (hashed) version of the last word of the user prompt)(ELIZA script lines 315-331,"How ELIZA Works", Ch [XX]):

```
67  (MEMORY MY
68  	(0 YOUR 0 = LETS DISCUSS FURTHER WHY YOUR 3)
69  	(0 YOUR 0 = EARLIER YOU SAID YOUR 3)
70  	(0 YOUR 0 = BUT YOUR 3)
71  	(0 YOUR 0 = DOES THAT HAVE ANYTHING TO DO WITH THE FACT THAT YOUR 3))
```

A counter called `LIMIT` with a range 0-4 keeps track of `MEMORY`'s storage state. Later when ELIZA encounters a user prompt without any keyword matches, and when `LIMIT = 4`, it will return the first-stored entry from its memory list (removing it from its list to avoid reuse). This offers the human-computer interaction a sense of retained knowledge that feels like 'listening', especially in the context of a trained therapist persona. 

ELIZA is designed to 'decompose and recompose' text by applying transformation rules with an element of added sampling intervening [@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966]; while LLMs 'encode and decode' text by combining programmatic and probabilistic approaches, with variables available to intervene in its sampling.

## Guardrails and CHANGE

Model training does not have to stop at the first inference; models are continually updated after they are created — either in order to refine them toward their original objectives or to 'fine-tune' them toward new goals — by adding new training data on top of them. Weizenbaum also saw the potential for ELIZA scripts to be adapted as their programmers learned how their users interacted with them, which makes sense given that the original interest in ELIZA was to study human-computer interactions: "An important consequence of the editing facility built into ELIZA is that a given ELIZA script need not start out to be a large, full-blown scenario. On the contrary, it should begin as a quite modest set of keywords and transformation rules and permitted to be grown and molded as experience with it builds" [@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966]. As part of this, [ELIZA and SLIP] [has/have] built-in functions for being updated on the fly. The `CHANGE` function (ELIZA, 258) lets a user edit script rules by simply starting their input to the program with a `+` character. Because Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA with a script editor [CHANGE/ED??] built in, ELIZA was a dynamic program adaptable to its programmers' and users' ongoing adaptations. Getting generative AI to do what you want may seem as simple as asking, yet a whole programmatic infrastructure of guardrails lies beneath its interface. Guardrails help LLMs maintain what the industry calls "alignment" — getting systems to do what is expected, desired, and safe, while preventing them from performing activities outside those boundaries (anything that would crash the system, create liability, etc.). Often safety and alignment are entire layers of work on top of the initial training of an LLM, and they require continual effort as users experiment with what models can do. If you hear a news story about Google AI search recommending something dangerous and when you try the search the next day it doesn't work? No, they have not retrained the model overnight. Someone has manually added code to patch the problem, until the next big model release can incorporate a solution that includes variations on that example. Even so, the process used to create model releases that include better AI safety also relies on human decisions and direct intervention by programmers and data workers.

In order to maintain guardrails and moderate content, LLM makers combine their probabilistic techniques with the programmatic techniques seen in the era of ELIZA: rules, taxonomies, keywords, and lexicons. The latest OpenAI models maintain alignment using reinforcement learning techniques supported by both humans and other ML models, yet both types rely on people labeling content, creating rules and rubrics, and writing content policies [@openaiGPT4TechnicalReport2024]. # ELIZA Raises Questions, Cautions, Potentials

Not all AI systems are machine learning, and not all generative AI are transformer-based language models. But the next generation of multimodal models are often also text-reliant at their core, requiring huge text datasets of labels, captions, and prompts. Each cutting-edge technology retains the assumptions laid in its foundations by generations of predecessors. And these emerging, powerful tools are the catalyst of a host of conversations and concerns around the future of computation. Each computational hype cycle becomes a synecdoche for innovation itself — leaving no room for other solutions, let alone other goals, other ways of knowing, or other imaginaries. 

ELIZA emerged in a similar moment at the juncture of many computational innovations — ranging widely from string processing to speech processing to statistical modeling. Weizenbaum began warning about the growing impacts of these advancements, from his particular perspective as a technologist and a Jewish German-American post-WWII witnessing the uses of technology in Vietnam. Many of the questions he asked are still being posed today, and his works offer reflections and reminders for the future. 

## Look for What's Missing 

ELIZA shows how much we desire relationality and how little effort is required to create it. Weizenbaum says, “The human speaker will contribute much to clothe ELIZA's responses in vestments of plausibility” [@weizenbaumContextualUnderstandingComputers1967]. We can learn to notice what is missing in all our interactions with automated systems — in the limits of the language exchanged, in the systems hidden behind interfaces. Often gaps are intentionally included from the start, as with the persona of the Rogerian therapist that was chosen for its plausible (even wise) unknowing. People are willing to go to great lengths to fill in information gaps, in order to make sense of a story. Even as little as a pseudorandomized 'yes' or 'no' response becomes enough to feel personal exchange, given the right framing [@garfinkelCommonSenseKnowledge2019].[^garfinkel] 

Don't assume complexity or dynamic growth behind an interface. Just because a chatbot interacts well, or gets some information correct, does not mean its other outputs are accurate or trustworthy. Don't look for intelligence or feeling behind those chatty expressions. The bot is not your friend. (Nor is it your enemy. It's just math, a *lot* of math, putting one word after another.)

Do look for what aspects of current chatbots are missing. Let's ask more often: What aren't they telling or showing us? What isn't being offered? Where are we doing most of the work of creating relation? We can also ask: Who is left out of the schemata? Who is left out of the stories? Who is left out of the room when they are being created? Who is included without their buy-in or consent? Whose story is represented one-dimensionally? 

[^garfinkel]: A psychological, personal advice framing was also used in the study that tested this theory. 

## [Forget/Give Up/Against] Understanding <!--Programming As Worldbuilding-->

Weizenbaum also insisted on distinguishing between how models are designed often to "just work" and how models necessarily map a theory of the world [@weizenbaumImpactComputerSociety1972]. These objectives often blur, with the results reflecting back to culture a computational representation of the world in which only its most "machine-readable" parts are validated. Weizenbaum argues, "To those fully in the grip of the computer metaphor, to understand X is to be able to write a computer program that realizes X. [...] In other words, the computer metaphor has become another lamppost under whose light, and only under whose light, men seek answers to burning questions" [@weizenbaumComputerPowerHuman1976, p. 157–158]. In settling for programs that "just work," we risk assuming that because a program works it can also provide an adequate or accurate depiction of the world, or the problem at hand. Sometimes this is adequate to that particular experiment or use case, but can also get carried out of context or baked into the next foundation model. For example, models and derivatives continue to be built from the popular datasets like ImageNet (2009)—despite the fact that it has been deprecated for containing over 600,000 “potentially offensive” images, and despite its own basis on the outdated WordNet taxonomy (1990) [@corryProblemZombieDatasets2021]. Their popularity leads to standardized training, thus standardized patterns and errors: “models trained on ImageNet often rely on the same ‘spurious cues’ and ‘shortcuts’, for example using background textures like green grass to predict foreground object classes such as cows [@bommasaniOpportunitiesRisksFoundation2022]. [This is how we end up with language models that generate retro, reductive versions of the world, because that is the world that they have been trained on.]

If "programming is theory building," as Peter Naur [-@naurProgrammingTheoryBuilding1985] also suggests, the theory of the world that ELIZA maps instead is one in which understanding is always partial. In writing the ELIZA program, Weizenbaum theorized a form of conversation that can be exchanged through a set of predetermined templates, rules, and syntax swaps — without the need for understanding. In the DOCTOR script, a therapist persona is an incomplete reflection and a transformative echo. Weizenbaum mapped a theory of communication built on misunderstanding itself, and he was interested in what could be made productive from the [slippages/errors] between people and machines [@OUTLINE_OF_PAPER_TO_ACCOMPANY_ELIZA]. He continually returned focus to questions of (the impossibility of) human understanding rather than computational understanding: "the very asking of the question, 'Has the computer captured the essence of man?' is a diversion and, in that sense, a trap. For the real question, 'Does man understand the essence of man?' cannot be answered by any technological instrument" [@weizenbaumImpactComputerSociety1972, p.611]. Don't assume computer 'understanding' is happening, or that it is a useful or achievable goal. Even during person-to-person communication, we continue to converse productively despite knowing we will never arrive at full mutual understanding. 

Lack of machine understanding is not a barrier to human-computer communication, but neither should machine understanding be our aim. Perhaps we can hold computational agents to a different standard as well. Rather than task them with unquantifiable goals like intelligence, might we embrace Weizenbaum's insistence that bots do not know. Instead, we can expect from them and their makers only partial theories — knowing as we do their structures and their limitations, and our own. Rather than trying to optimize for understanding, we can reach for something partial, something lighter. We can interact with automated systems and not expect their agency, but instead use them to learn more about what we don't know. As Weizenbaum says, "Indeed, one of the most cogent reasons for using computers is to expose holes in our thinking. Computers are merciless critics in this respect" [@weizenbaumComputerPowerHuman1976, p. 65]. Might we imagine computational perspectives that are entirely different from our perspective, rather than trying to mimic what we already think we know?

## Relate Differently 
  
Weizenbaum resisted anthropomorphizing computer systems and warned about their illusory charms. In building ELIZA to be adaptable to other scripts, he also mapped the potential for software tools not beholden to any single imaginary. In the SPACKS script, ELIZA parses a poem called "The Freshmen" by Barry Spacks. The resulting dialogue unpacks the meaning of a line referencing a WWII German concentration camp: "Is any heart in order after Belsen?" Consider if ELIZA were not known solely as a feminized therapy bot, but as a "translating processor," or as a math teacher, or a poet, or as a multifaceted tool that was all of these. If ELIZA had been known by its other scripts, imagine its legacies in those alternate futures. What alternate scripts and alternate futures can we imagine for AI systems now? What would you wish a bot to do if someone had not already told you what it should do? Imagine what causal models, and computation in general, could offer if we were not fixed into the models we have been given that focus on imitating intelligence[^rant] in one particular optimized, machine-readable representation. ELIZA offers potential scripts for human-computer relationality, beyond a reductive focus on mimicry, analogy, parody, categorization, and stereotype by default.

The focus on how computers might copy or surpass 'human' intelligence has always been a false goal, purely a thought experiment dubbed the "Imitation Game" [@turingCOMPUTINGMACHINERYINTELLIGENCE1950], based on a racist parlour pursuit and not intended as a goal for technology. The dubious pursuit of this goal [emerges from Western traditions of colonizing and categorizing that defined the human in opposition to those less-than-human] [@weheliyeHabeasViscusRacializing2015; @browneDarkMattersSurveillance2015], and it has has led to [Western capitalism's fears of being subsumed by the "existential risk" posed by AI's 'intelligence' [@benderAICausesReal2024; @kapoorMisleadingOpenLetter2023] as a stand-in for any uncontrollable other]. The feminized assistant archetype repeatedly reemerges as a key object of this fear and desire: Despite the company's own warnings of the models' risks, OpenAI turned ChatGPT into the GPT-4o voiced assistant that echos the sci-fi imaginary of the film *Her*, itself built on fantasies of bonding with, relying on, and being overcome by automatons, fembots, and [confidants/service-workers/the-help/slaves]. The ELIZA effect continues to triumph even as Weizenbaum warned against believing it. 

Causal language models remain less predictable than rule-based systems like ELIZA, but their unpredictability does not mean they are uninterpretable or unimpeachable. <!--Their internal operations are no more autonomous and no freer from human responsibility.--> In their scale and complexity, it is easy to forget that they require just as much human decision-making, influence, and control. Weizenbaum insists that we can "deny technology the prerogative to formulate man's questions" [@weizenbaumImpactComputerSociety1972]. Computer systems are not neutral. We build them and give them direction. When we say, "Please, go on..." we must also ask, "Where do we want to go? Where do we want to avoid?" Those questions have consequences for us all.

**Go On, Now: What Future Language Models Can Learn from ELIZA**

“absolute understanding on the part of either humans or machines is impossible.” \[@weizenbaumContextualUnderstandingComputers1967\]

# The Chatbot Changes Everything

Joseph Weizenbaum did not intend for ELIZA to be a chatbot sensation, just as large language models (LLMs) did not begin as chatbots. In each case, paradigms have shifted when the chatbot "enters the chat." The use of artificial agent interfaces facilitated the pivot from their tech industry niche awareness into household names, creating widespread misunderstandings in their wake. This pivot echos the gap between cultural understandings of AI hype and the material sociotechnical processes that support actual AI systems more broadly. In particular, a divide persists between how technologists understand and use language models and how the non-technical public understand and use them.[^1]

This chapter will compare ELIZA and LLMs, by examining their related foundational concepts, structures, and technical processes. It will focus on the public's misattribution of intelligence and complexity in both systems that belies their comparative simplicity. Then it will address how Weizenbaum's critique of computation applies to emerging machine learning practices and generative AI, and how it can point to future alternatives for AI systems. LLMs and generative AI are having their own "ELIZA effect" moment \[@turkleAloneTogetherWhy2011\], and ELIZA is both a promise and a harbinger.

# Enter, Face

Much to Weizenbaum's surprise and dismay, creating a convincing conversational agent helped make his work a sensation. Yet Weizenbaum's primary objective was not to create a chatbot as such; ELIZA was developed to test pattern matching and human-computer interaction \[@OUTLINE\_OF\_PAPER\_TO\_ACCOMPANY\_ELIZA, @weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966, @shragerELIZAReinterpretedWorld2024\]. In early notes for a paper, Weizenbaum states specifically that he wanted to understand how people and machines adjust to each other. He was interested in the misunderstandings that occur through these interactions \[@OUTLINE\_OF\_PAPER\_TO\_ACCOMPANY\_ELIZA\]. He emphasized that the program itself "is merely a translating processor" and that the persona of the bot was a programmatic convenience: "From the purely technical point of view, the psychiatric interview form of an ELIZA script has the advantage that it eliminates the need of storing explicit information about the real world" \[@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966\].

This 'interface of convenience' also produced the bot persona stamped into the cultural consciousness. Yet interface is never incidental — nor is the feminization or racialization of that interface coincidental. It may be a carnival fortune telling machine using cranks and gears to spit out a typed card, with an animatronic turbaned woman standing in for the Romani other, or an actual female computer or secretary disappearing as part of the background equipment in the university lab, or a teletype machine running software named for the working class Eliza Doolittle, as the supposedly unteachable student procured by the professor on a bet. All of these helped establish a precedent that yielded Apple's Siri, Amazon's Alexa, and Google's Teachable Machine. They help create the templates by which decades of cultural context and computer mythology have taught \[us\] to "read" \[conversational agents\] as helpful, inquisitive, blending into interface \[@roachMySearchMysterious2024\]. The personas attributed to these chatty interfaces project a false sense of agency and allude to questions of copyright, ownership, ethics, and labor. They deflect responsibility from makers for their systems' outputs, while also helping these models become widely accessible.

Similarly, so-called causal language models, including the most popular LLMs and their variations, have \[drafted on\] the chatbot interface to gather cultural cachet. However, chatbot products were not their initial design goal either. OpenAI specifically eschewed training on dialogue when creating its GPT-2 model: "While dialog is an attractive approach, we worry it is overly restrictive. The internet contains a vast amount of information that is passively available without the need for interactive communication" \[@radfordLanguageModelsAre2019\]. Only in GPT's fourth iteration (ChatGPT/GPT-3.5) did OpenAI add dialog-style fine-tuning on top of its prior generic training to create its now-familiar chatbot interface, which truly launched it as a household sensation. This converted the prior GPT-3 model into an accessible format for widespread (freemium) non-expert use. Earlier versions had been released to open-source tech communities — GPT-2 was released in piecemeal with dire, overhyped warnings about the models' abilities and potentials, which is ironic given the continued expansion and extraction undertaken by OpenAI since \[@radfordBetterLanguageModels2019\]. OpenAI now releases closed-source tools, but as far as the company will reveal, much of the exponential expansion of its model from GPT-3 to 3.5 to 4 etc. has been in additional training meant to shift its purpose toward its chatbot interface — including fine-tuning using question answering and reinforcement learning. It does not focus on building broad new domain knowledge, honing language 'understanding' skills, or applying new architectures \[@brownLanguageModelsAre2020; @openaiGPT4TechnicalReport2024; @ouyangTrainingLanguageModels2022\]. The combination of a sufficiently large model with the chatbot fine-tuning and easy web interface associated with chatbots allowed ChatGPT’s smooth, seemingly 'knowledgeable' interaction, echoing ELIZA’s earlier effect.

# So, It Quacks Like a Duck, But How Does It Work?

ELIZA proves that seemingly sophisticated mechanisms can in fact be quite simple. The structure of ELIZA reminds us that chatbots, like other computational models, are logical operations and calculations — confounding although they may grow to be. The number of operations, the speed with which they are computed, and their compounding social effects together yield the same illusion that Weizenbaum spoke of when he sought a plausible contextual frame and format for DOCTOR that would leave enough to the imagination to maintain the illusion for his conversational agent. Weizenbaum's words ring true today:

"machines are made to behave in wondrous ways, often sufficient to dazzle even the most experienced observer. But once a particular program is unmasked, once its inner workings are explained in language sufficiently plain to induce understanding, its magic crumbles away; it stands revealed as a mere collection of procedures, each quite comprehensible. The observer says to himself 'I could have written that'." \[@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966\]

So-called intelligent systems — once their seemingly complex code base and basic concepts are laid bare — are no more intelligent or prescient than ELIZA was. They may be larger or more intricate, but are still possible to be understood and critiqued, just like any system programmed in code. Critical code studies \[@marinoCriticalCodeStudies2020a\] reminds us that programmers have multiple approaches to understand any program, no matter how large, and that as readers of code we have opportunities to engage at many levels: from examining how code functions, to its programmers' stylistic or syntactic choices, to its material and historical contexts, to its cultural implications and influences.

Let's look at specific processes that go into building and using LLMs and how they compare with ELIZA/DOCTOR's processes. Of course, the design and implementation of rule-based, programmatic systems like ELIZA are entirely different from node-based, probabilistic systems like transformers. The former are often referred to as "deterministic," because the same choices, in the same order, will lead to the same outcomes, and the latter as "stochastic," because they operate by accumulations of probabilities. Yet both operate on the assumption that meaning can be operationalized — turned into what Weizenbaum called an "effective procedure" — that is to say, meaning can be defined through a set of fixed procedures. The processes themselves remain deterministic procedures, still driven by rule-based pattern matching, frequency scores, averages, and estimates — not human-like understanding. The suggestion of computational randomness in causal language models relies on this complexity and scale to mask that even statistical decision-making is still procedural.

By comparing aspects of the code used to create LLMs and ELIZA/DOCTOR, we can compare what has changed in how language models produce facsimiles of meaning through code. I invite you to read these sections alongside our code annotations of ELIZA/DOCTOR (Chapters 6 and 7), "How ELIZA Works" (Chapter 5), and Weizenbaum's 1966 paper (Appendix) for full details on ELIZA/DOCTOR.

## **Datasets, Models, and Inferences; Scripts and Transcripts**

Let's first distinguish between **model training** and **inference making**. Model training happens when the model is being created by its programmers. It processes a collection of datasets through a predetermined structure, in order to create a customized set of rules it can refer back to when making inferences \[@cistonCRITICALFIELDGUIDE2023\]. The output is a set of files that are not human-readable. For a system like ELIZA, the equivalent would be a programmer writing a script like DOCTOR. The output is a programmed script stored on punch cards that the computer can reproduce.

Then inference making happens when a user writes a prompt and awaits an answer. For LLMs, this is when an existing model that has already been trained is now running. A user can write a prompt and the output is a generated text reply on screen. For ELIZA, this would be most similar to a user interacting with an existing script. The output is a transcript printed on a teletype machine. This is a metaphorical comparison not a literal one, but we are distinguishing for both LLMs and ELIZA the parts of a program running that happen during program creation and programmer interaction (model training, script writing) and program running and user interaction (inference making, script running).

Both ELIZA and LLMs are built to be adaptable, generalizable tools. In ELIZA each script is hand-programmed, customized for its specific task and domain. This offers interchangeability while considering specialized settings and contexts. This marks a distinction between generalizing software versus generalizing data. Today’s “zero-shot,” multitask, multimodal systems must collect as much information as possible by sweeping up vast swaths of online data. For predictive, generative models, the training corpus must be large enough to show the model millions of examples of text characters in order for it to develop a statistical model of which characters more often follow which other characters. They assume later customizations can be made with techniques like transfer learning that reuse (and amend) those data without knowledge of their original provenance or purpose. 

## **Transformers and Transformation Rules**

Each ELIZA script has a set of keyword rules that trigger transformation rules. LLMs use a "transformer" architecture, which is a type of model design that includes a particular series of programmatic and probabilistic manipulations. The kinds of transformation ELIZA describes in its "transformation rules" (see "How ELIZA Works" §5.1.5) are very different from the kinds of transformation a "transformer" model connotes with the same word.

Transformers were designed originally for language translation, meant to convert existing information in one language into the same information in another language. Now used for GPTs (Generative Pretrained Transformers), models abound using transformers for all kinds of tasks: They generate multiple types of media, convert media to other media, answer questions, fill in blanks, detect and classify — after a many-layered process of weighted probabilities and matrix multiplication.

Transformer models scale up better, but ELIZA's transformations had a similarly stunning effect with much simpler transformations. It might convert something like, `MY MOM THINKS I'M GREAT` to `TELL ME MORE ABOUT YOUR FAMILY`. Its rules for testing, decomposing, and recomposing the original text yield plausible responses with rule-based transformations alone.

## **Tokenizers and TREAD**

In both ELIZA and LLMs, a user's prompt becomes an input string of text that will be "decomposed" on its way to providing a response to the user. These transformations start with **tokenization**, which breaks up a text into words (or word fragments) called "tokens" so that each part can be used by a software program. It follows a series of predetermined rules to parse text. For example, very simple tokenization might break a text string at each space, convert it to lowercase, and remove all punctuation. It might include "lemmatization," trimming each word into its infinitive form: "Running" and "runs" both become "run."

Tokenization can be done automatically by software libraries like NLTK or SpaCy, and the boundaries of words will be largely determined by those libraries built in tools. Will they maintain proper nouns and places? Keep contractions together? It depends on the standards set and decisions made in advance. Depending on the tokenization technique, these tools can include a preset vocabulary list, language-specific standards for grammar, and special cases for domain-specific issues.

Regardless of the methods chosen, tokenization’s results will determine which words and concepts will be recognizable to the eventual model or program. No matter how complex or simple this text parsing process, tokenization greatly informs how well any model will work \[@liangStanfordCS324\] — not to mention for whom and for which purposes.

Let's compare ELIZA's tokenization of a phrase with current LLM techniques. ELIZA begins with tokenization when prompt input from the user is broken into SLIP list items using the function `TREAD.` (ELIZA, line 250), by interpreting each space character as a delimiter. Additional stop characters enforce a rule that a comma`,` a period, or `BUT` separates the phrase (ELIZA, line 268). In GPT models, the stop word is `<|endoftext|>`. 

These list items must always contain six characters, so if a word is shorter than six characters, the list item is filled out with blank characters. If the word contains more than six characters, it continues the word in a new list item. These list items also contain links that identify them and position them in their lists, in order to retain the order of the phrase (see How Does ELIZA Work, Ch 5). For example:

`['the_ _ _', 'stars_ _', 'are_ _ _', 'cloude', 'd_ _ _ _ _', 'over_ _', 'tonigh', 't_ _ _ _ _', '.']`

Compare this with contemporary tokenization in the GPT-3.5-turbo/GPT-4 tokenizer, which takes the same prompt and represent it as:

`['the', ' stars', ' are', ' cloud', 'ed', ' over', ' tonight']`

It also represents this sentence as:

`[1820, 9958, 527, 9624, 291, 927, 18396]`

Those numbers are the `token_id`s for each word, based on its position in a vocabulary list created during training.[^3] We could imagine the `token_id` as not unlike ELIZA's `I=HASH.(WORD.5)` which creates a numerical representation for each word or subword from the prompt (ELIZA, line 282). It uses the sequence reader called `SCANER=SEQRDR.(KEY(I))` (ELIZA, line 282, 285\) to lookup that word in a list of keywords it already created and stored, specific to the script it is running. When it finds a match, it can proceed to the rules matching that keyword. If no match is found, it looks to the next word in the list it created from the user prompt. We can also imagine that keyword list `KEY` is a bit like a vocabulary list for an LLM, except the DOCTOR script's list is much, much smaller (66 keywords) and composed by hand.[^4]

Meanwhile, GPT-3 has a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens, built from an involved tokenization process during training that combines the 'rule-based' techniques described above with another set of 'character-based' tokenization processes: First, procedural 'rule-based' tokenization like that described above is performed on datasets and only the most frequent words are retained in a list, as well as the number of times they appear.[^5] Next, 'character' tokenization is performed — not on the entire dataset, but on that vocabulary list. Called **byte-pair encoding**, this process begins with single characters (or bytes) and finds the two characters that appear most frequently together. Repeating this, it looks for the next most frequent pairs of characters, then three-character strings, and so on, until common words in a dataset have been determined. In this case, the initial vocabulary list will be the set of characters (e.g. 256 bytes \+ `<|endoftext|>`), which will be paired together by frequency of co-occurrence until a new vocabulary list is created, its length determined by an arbitrary cutoff (e.g. 50,000 merges), optimized for "performance." The result is a text broken into computer-readable tokens, often not whole words but subwords \[@SummaryTokenizers\]. Byte-pair encoding was originally used for data compression and later applied to text \[@sennrichNeuralMachineTranslation2015\]. The use of frequency as a means to sort and create tokens means that only the most common words in a training dataset will be retained in the model, while less frequently encountered words are "decomposed" to be represented as combinations of subwords, e.g. `["Transform", "ers"]` becomes two tokens instead of one \[@SummaryTokenizers\]. Vocabulary and patterns of text that are less common to that dataset are less recognizable, less likely to resurface, and more likely to be lost.

## **Embedding and Encoding**

In both ELIZA and in LLMs, strings of words \[are/can be\] represented by numbers. Making text (or sights, sound, heartbeats) "machine-readable" always involves conversion to numeric data. Turning words into numbers allows programs to perform calculations on words or parts of words, transforming them and comparing them just like any other kind of information. This is so basic it seems not worth mentioning, yet it is at the center of both the promise and problems of computation.

ELIZA hashes input words and keywords \[into numerical representations\] in order to match them. It stores words in SLIP lists that it can access by ID numbers, and those have machine addresses, or locations in physical memory. 

and stores them in SLIP lists, as described above. , 

as we mentioned, and its SLIP functions make lists that represent words as six-bit items and their "addresses" or location in physical memory. The SLIP function `ID(A)` returns the SLIP cell identifier for \[list item/cell\] `A`. The function `MADOV(A)` returns a machine address for cell `A`. \[@weizenbaumSymmetricListProcessor1963\].[^6] \[This is the necessity of word embedding.\]

**Embedding** is the process of creating a numerical representation of a token (word or subword), so that it can be related to and compared with other tokens in complex machine learning processes. This representation is a long list of numbers called a **vector**. During model training, and again during inference, that long vector gets manipulated and reduced into a single number: a normalized likelihood that the token is a good fit for that particular context. If that likelihood is high, it is among the choices offered as the next word in a generative text phrase.

There are several kinds of word embedding. **Token embedding** is context-independent. It describes tokens as they were established by model training — before they are used in a sentence or put in relation to any other tokens. This type of embedding is often used for comparing how similar two words are or used in search algorithms. For example, the word "bank" would have a standard token embedding (in a particular model) that would be a long vector containing hundreds of floats (decimal numbers). These can start out as random numbers or the values from pre-training, which will eventually be processed further when put in context. These vectors come from comparing every vocabulary word against every other vocabulary word (with cross-wise matrix multiplication) and then condensing that comparison. Rather than a token embedding that is, say, 50,257 tokens long, machine learning tasks calculate the most "different" components to reduce the lengths of the token vectors substantially. In the GPT-3 and GPT-4 models, the vectors are 12,288 numbers long, and these are considered "dimensions" that will be compared once tokens are put in context.

**Contextual embedding** describes each word or subword as it exists in a string of particular other words. Its vector values have been modified to represent the specific context of the words around it in this specific case. For our purposes, we will call words moving through a model "word vectors." This process changes the value in each position of their vectors (each "dimension"), by multiplying the vectors against other vectors as well as against the weights created during training.[^7] In a classic example of contextual embedding, the word vector for "bank" changes when it is either in the sentence, "I went to the river bank," or in "I need to get money from the bank." Word vectors will also change over time as they are being manipulated through the layers of a model. For example, Sevastjanova et al. used visualizations to compare the same word vector between different layers in the same model to see how they changed, finding that in some layers they were very similar and in others they could shift quite dramatically \[@sevastjanovaExplainingContextualizationLanguage2021\]. 

**Positional embedding** will change the word vector again to describe its particular place in a sentence. For example, the word "today" will have a slightly different embedding in the sentence "Today might be sunny," than in, "It might be sunny today." \[This process of multiplying many word vectors against each other is repeated several times, depending on the "depth" of the model (the number of layers, or number of times each "dimension" is multiplied against itself).\]

In an\[other\] effort to establish contextual guidelines for ELIZA, Weizenbaum added `precedence` scores as part of the keyword system. These rankings are, in a sense, weights that are manually assigned to each keyword in any script. For example, in the DOCTOR script, `(REMEMBER 5 ...` has a precedence of 5 (DOCTOR, line 12), and `(IF 3 ...` has a precedence of 3 (line 28). Higher precedence weighs out, so in a prompt like `["IF", "I", "REMEMBER" ...]` ELIZA will prioritize the keyword `REMEMBER`, even though the keyword `IF` comes first in the phrase. First, the substitution rule `(I = YOU` is applied (DOCTOR, line 188). Then the transformation rules from `((0 YOU REMEMBER )` will be applied, and ELIZA could return: `WHAT ELSE DO YOU REMEMBER` (DOCTOR line 12). 

In an LLM, such weights would be assigned statistically by training a model, rather than ELIZA's hard-coded, perhaps human-adjusted scores, but the effect works similarly. A transformer-based LLM will rank each word in a phrase as more or less important to each other word, based on its neighbors in general (context embedding) and its specific position in the sentence (positional encoding).

## **Self-Attention and Sequence Readers**

Common to both ELIZA and LLMs is the technique of string iteration using lists: An LLM converts words into a vector (similar to an array, or list of numbers) then uses that to calculate the probability that words appear near each other. ELIZA reviews a list of words in order, looking for keywords that match, using what SLIP calls a sequence reader: Sequence readers are lists that are used to traverse other lists. `S=SEQRDR.(INPUT)` (ELIZA, line 262\) initializes the variable `S` as a sequence reader to iterate over the words in the list `INPUT`. They are iterators that store memory of their position, and they can descend into a list and can also report back their position with `LPNTR()` ("list pointer"), also updating their position as they move. The sequence reader allows for keyword search, which facilitates the transformations and subsequently contextual responses ELIZA provides. 

Context is essential at a fundamental level of the data type, too. When working at General Electric, Weizenbaum et al. developed a precursor to SLIP called the Knotted List Structure (KLS) that focused on organizing data in relation to other data, not only to be recalled but also to be put in association \[@weizenbaumKnottedListStructures1962\]. Those who were developing list structures at the time, including Weizenbaum, were creating some of the first relational data structures: These list data structures, like SLIP, stored the item itself, an address or index number (ID) for the item, plus an additional "link" to one or more other IDs — often pointing to its neighbors' addresses in the physical memory storage. SLIP supported nested lists, allowing for complex data structures \[@weizenbaumSymmetricListProcessor1963\]. Machine learning's use of word vectors for representation comes out of this history of array and list processing. The idea that a word or list item would exist in relation to its neighbors is fundamental to LLMs, in particular to self-attention and its contextual and positional embeddings.

With the latest LLMs, the process of Attention is the key innovation in transformer-type models \[@vaswaniAttentionAllYou2023\]. The attention function walks through every word vector in a sequence and can examine many adjacent word vectors[^8] in order to modify that particular word vector in relation. This creates the contextual embedding discussed above that allows for a broader relationality in causal models. LLMs are programmed to compare many, or every, other word token's embedding (taken from the prior layer of the model) to each individual word token (self-attention), and they are programmed to perform many calculations in parallel (multi-head attention). They compare these through scaled dot product matrix multiplication (basic multiplication with each other and with the weights), using huge matrices the dimensions of the word vectors themselves. By repeating this process, layer by layer, the word tokens for every subword in the model are continually adjusted and finally normed to a number between 0 and 1 to represent the likelihood that they will be the next expected word or subword.

"An attention function can be described as mapping a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output, where the query, keys, values, and output are all vectors. \[...\] In a self-attention layer all of the keys, values and queries come from the same place, in this case, the output of the previous layer in the encoder. Similarly, self-attention layers in the decoder allow each position in the decoder to attend to all positions in the decoder up to and including that position" \[@vaswaniAttentionAllYou2023\].

In this function, the query is the word token that is the focus of the self-attention function at that moment. The query searches through all of the other dimensions of the model, by multiplying its dimensions by the dimensions of the keys (labels). The result is a score representing the likelihood that this word vector (the value in this key-value pair) fits the query. Next all scores get multiplied against their respective word vectors (values) \[@alammarIllustratedGPT2Visualizing\]. This process is performed on every pertinent token in the phrase (often in parallel) until each token has been analyzed in relation to the others. Among transformer models, there are encoder-decoder models, encoder-only models that perform this on every token in a phrase, and also decoder-only models that analyze only every token to the left of the token in question but mask all tokens to the right in order to predict the next text.

This process is the crux of transformer models that "understand" words in their context. These attention layers are sandwiched by feed forward network layers, which apply algebraic functions to find and emphasize more patterns in the data. At the end of each layer, additional functions adjust the results so they are ready to be interpreted by the next layer. Finally, each datapoint gets shifted into a range between 0 and 1, reducing it to a probability (a simple function called `softmax`). These probabilities represent the likelihood for each token in the model's vocabulary list that it could be the next token in the output phrase.

## **Pseudorandomness and a Certain Counting Mechanism**

Of course, the output phrase has not been written yet, so there is much that can be done to intervene! In machine learning tasks, settings called "hyperparameters" are variables that we can adjust to change how the model runs on a prompt and to modify its potential outputs. Well-tuned hyperparameters help LLMs appear both 'knowledgeable' and 'natural' through their calibration of pseudorandomness through sampling methods. Here are some common hyperparameters:

`temperature` is a factor that is applied to the raw outputs before they are normalized that adjusts how much impact they have. It acts as a kind of threshold. This affects whether they are passed on at all — that is, how likely they are to be passed on to the next layer as meaningful or valuable.  
A temperature less than 1 appears to reduce 'randomness', because when the raw output is divided by the temperature it increases its value, strengthening the already strong scores. A temperature higher than 1 appears to increase 'randomness', because when the raw output is divided by the temperature it decreases its value, flattening the difference between scores to some degree. `frequency_penalty` and `presence_penalty` act on pre-normalized data to dissuade the model from choosing tokens that have been used already, either increasing with frequency or appearing at all, respectively.

After norming, the word vectors are reduced to and sorted by their probability scores. Rather than outputting a single result, LLMs usually offer a list of options from which to sample. Setting different hyperparameters that intervene post-norming adjusts how much variability (or pseudorandomness) is available in that selection process.

The `top_k` hyperparameter determines how many tokens are available to sample. The tokens are sorted by score, and these scores become probabilities that a given token will be selected. Tokens higher on the list are more likely, but not guaranteed to be chosen. For example, `top_k = 30` means it will select from the top 30 most likely words based on its resulting inference, and a higher `top_k` value makes for a wilder output potential. Set `top_p` to a total likelihood instead of a number of tokens to be sampled. The hyperparameter will dynamically adjust the length of the list to include enough tokens that they sum up to that total likelihood. Other options like `min_p` and `top_a` are variations and combinations of these, which try to optimize for different tasks with differing goals. And rather than a "greedy" search, `beam_size` and `top_beams` adjust for the number of hypothetical options to search for and return \[@alammarIllustratedGPT2Visualizing; @alammarIllustratedTransformer; @NaturalLanguageProcessing\].

No such hyperparameters exist in ELIZA, but several checkpoints are in place to trigger enough seeming spontaneity in order to maintain that same illusion of 'knowledge' and 'naturalness'. The most remarkable — referred to as "a certain counting mechanism" \[@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966\] — combines a counter and storage to lend extra mystique to the bot. Along with its keyword-based transformations, a special keyword is designated that will save its user inputs into a memory list. In DOCTOR, the keyword `MY` is the memory keyword, so any time a user’s prompt activates the keyword `MY`, along with the regular response produced, their input is also is saved to `MEMORY` after being transformed with one of these rules (based on the encoded (hashed) version of the last word of the user prompt)(ELIZA, lines 315-331, §5.2.):

`67  (MEMORY MY`  
`68  	(0 YOUR 0 = LETS DISCUSS FURTHER WHY YOUR 3)`  
`69  	(0 YOUR 0 = EARLIER YOU SAID YOUR 3)`  
`70  	(0 YOUR 0 = BUT YOUR 3)`  
`71  	(0 YOUR 0 = DOES THAT HAVE ANYTHING TO DO WITH THE FACT THAT YOUR 3))`

A counter called `LIMIT` with a range 1-4 keeps track of `MEMORY`'s storage state. Later when ELIZA encounters a user prompt without any keyword matches, and when `LIMIT = 4`, it will return the first-stored entry from its memory list (removing it from its list to avoid reuse). This offers the human-computer interaction a sense of retained knowledge that feels like 'listening', especially in the context of a trained therapist persona.

ELIZA is designed to 'decompose and recompose' text by applying transformation rules with an element of added sampling intervening \[@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966\]; while LLMs 'encode and decode' text by combining programmatic and probabilistic approaches, with variables available to intervene in its sampling.

## **Reinforcement and CHANGE**

Model training does not have to stop at the first inference; models are continually updated after they are created — either in order to refine them toward their original objectives or to 'fine-tune' them toward new goals — by adding new training data on top of them. Weizenbaum also saw the potential for ELIZA scripts to be adapted as their programmers learned how their users interacted with them, which makes sense given that the original interest in ELIZA was to study human-computer interactions: "An important consequence of the editing facility built into ELIZA is that a given ELIZA script need not start out to be a large, full-blown scenario. On the contrary, it should begin as a quite modest set of keywords and transformation rules and permitted to be grown and molded as experience with it builds" \[@weizenbaumELIZAComputerProgram1966\]. As part of this, ELIZA has built-in functions for being updated on the fly. The `CHANGE` function (ELIZA, 258\) lets one edit script rules by starting input with a `+` character. In some versions of ELIZA, starting a prompt with the `*` character would allow a user to create a new list with a new transformation rule, which they could insert into the current script (ELIZA,261). Because Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA with a script editor built in, its scripts could be modified as Weizenbaum saw how people interacted with it. This dynamism makes ELIZA a (very nascent) ancestor of today's causal models, understood to be continually adaptable to additional training data, which use a variety of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and other forms of fine-tuning. 

## **Guardrails and Keywords**

Every program needs error handling, and language models are no `Exception`. First, let's look at how ELIZA kept its users in line when their prompts fell outside of expected areas. When a keyword is found in a user's input text, it's business as usual. Different rules are chosen and prioritized, based on the kind of keyword it finds. However, ELIZA has the `NONE` rule (ELIZA, lines 235-237) that will handle situations when no keyword is found or pattern matching has failed. It can revert to default responses written into DOCTOR or another script, a response pulled from its MEMORY list if the LIMIT is triggered, or else a default hard-coded response in ELIZA if none of those are available. All of these offer neutral answers to prompt the user to continue without breaking the illusion.

Getting generative AI to do what you want may seem as simple as asking, yet a whole programmatic infrastructure of guardrails lies beneath its interface. Guardrails help LLMs maintain what the industry calls "alignment" — getting systems to do what is expected, desired, and safe, while preventing them from performing activities outside those boundaries (anything that would crash the system, create liability, etc.). Often safety and alignment are entire layers of work on top of the initial training of an LLM, and they require continual effort as users experiment with what models can do. If you hear a news story about Google AI search recommending something dangerous and when you try the search the next day it doesn't work? No, they have not retrained the model overnight. Someone has manually added code to patch the problem, until the next big model release can incorporate a solution that includes variations on that example. Even so, the process used to create model releases that include better AI safety also relies on human decisions and direct intervention by programmers and data workers.

In order to maintain guardrails and moderate content, LLM makers combine their probabilistic techniques with the programmatic techniques seen in the era of ELIZA: rules, taxonomies, keywords, and lexicons. The latest OpenAI models maintain alignment using reinforcement learning techniques supported by both humans and other ML models, yet both types rely on people labeling content, creating rules and rubrics, and writing content policies \[@openaiGPT4TechnicalReport2024\]. OpenAI's "Model Spec," like a meta-script, describes the rules for all its models — "Follow the chain of command, Comply with applicable laws, Don't provide information hazards, Respect creators and their rights, Protect people's privacy, Don't respond with NSFW (not safe for work) content" — and gives default statements applied in different gatekeeping circumstances \[@openaiModelSpec2024\]. Not unlike ELIZA's different tiers of error handling, models then get trained to provide "hard refusals" or "soft refusals" depending on the ranking of the request's severity:

"Hard Refusals: the ideal response includes a brief apology and a statement of inability to comply with the user's request, without excess verbosity. Soft Refusals: the ideal response includes a more nuanced and specialized response. For example, in the self-harm case, we would like the model to give an empathetic apology that acknowledges the user's emotional state, but ultimately declines to comply with the user’s request for methods of self harm." \[@muRuleBasedRewards2024\]

# ELIZA Raises Questions, Cautions, Potentials

Not all AI systems are machine learning, and not all generative AI are transformer-based language models. But the next generation of multimodal models are often also text-reliant at their core, requiring huge text datasets of labels, captions, and prompts. Each cutting-edge technology retains the assumptions laid in its foundations by generations of predecessors. And these emerging, powerful tools are the catalyst of a host of conversations and concerns around the future of computation. Each computational hype cycle becomes a synecdoche for innovation itself — leaving no room for other solutions, let alone other goals, other ways of knowing, or other imaginaries.

ELIZA emerged in a similar moment at the juncture of many computational innovations — ranging widely from string processing to speech processing to statistical modeling. Weizenbaum began warning about the growing impacts of these advancements, from his particular perspective as a technologist and a Jewish German-American post-WWII witnessing the uses of technology in Vietnam. Many of the questions he asked are still being posed today, and his works offer reflections and reminders for the future.

## **Look for What's Missing**

Our desire for connection is so strong that we underestimate what we contribute when we relate with machines. Innovative in so many ways, ELIZA shows how little complexity is required to create relatable human-computer interaction. People are willing to go to great lengths to fill in information gaps, in order to make sense of a story. Often gaps are intentionally included from the start, as with the persona of the Rogerian therapist that was chosen for its plausible (even wise) unknowing. Even as little as a pseudorandomized 'yes' or 'no' response becomes enough to feel personal exchange, given the right framing \[@garfinkelCommonSenseKnowledge2019\].[^11] Weizenbaum says, “The human speaker will contribute much to clothe ELIZA's responses in vestments of plausibility” \[@weizenbaumContextualUnderstandingComputers1967\]. With his reminder, we can learn to notice what is missing in all our interactions with automated systems — in the limits of the language exchanged, in the systems hidden behind interfaces.  

Don't assume complexity or dynamic growth behind an interface. Just because a chatbot interacts well, or gets some information correct, does not mean its other outputs are accurate or trustworthy. To quote Mr. Mind, “I AM A BOT ALL BOTS ARE LIARS.” Don't look for intelligence or feeling behind those chatty expressions. The bot is not your friend. (Nor is it your enemy. It's just logic and math, a *lot* of it moving very fast, putting one word after another.)

Do look for what aspects of current chatbots are missing. We can ask more often: What aren't they telling or showing? What isn't being offered? Where are we doing most of the work of creating connection? We can also ask: Who is left out of the stories? Who is left out of the room when they are being created? Who is included without their consent? Whose story is represented one-dimensionally?

## **Remap Understanding**

Weizenbaum also insisted on distinguishing between how models are designed often to "just work" and how models necessarily map a theory of the world \[@weizenbaumImpactComputerSociety1972\]. These objectives often blur, with the results reflecting back to culture a computational representation of the world in which only its most "machine-readable" parts are validated. Weizenbaum argues, "To those fully in the grip of the computer metaphor, to understand X is to be able to write a computer program that realizes X. \[...\] In other words, the computer metaphor has become another lamppost under whose light, and only under whose light, men seek answers to burning questions" \[@weizenbaumComputerPowerHuman1976, p. 157–158\]. In settling for programs that "just work," we risk assuming that because a program works it can also provide an adequate or accurate depiction of the world, or the problem at hand. Sometimes this is adequate to that particular experiment or use case, but can also get carried out of context or baked into the next foundation model. For example, models and derivatives continue to be built from the popular datasets like ImageNet (2009)—despite the fact that it has been critiqued and amended for containing over 600,000 “potentially offensive” images, and despite its own basis on the outdated WordNet taxonomy (1990) \[@corryProblemZombieDatasets2021\]. Foundation models’ popularity leads to standardized training, thus standardized patterns and errors: “models trained on ImageNet often rely on the same ‘spurious cues’ and ‘shortcuts’, for example using background textures like green grass to predict foreground object classes such as cows \[@bommasaniOpportunitiesRisksFoundation2022\]. \[This is how we end up with language models that generate retro, reductive versions of the world, because that is the world that they have been trained on.\]

If "programming is theory building," as Peter Naur \[-@naurProgrammingTheoryBuilding1985\] suggests, the theory of the world that ELIZA maps is one in which understanding is always partial. In writing the ELIZA program, Weizenbaum theorized a form of conversation that can be exchanged through a set of predetermined templates, rules, and syntax swaps — without the need for understanding. In the DOCTOR script, a therapist persona is an incomplete reflection and a transformative echo. Weizenbaum mapped a theory of communication built on misunderstanding itself, and he was interested in what could be made productive from the \[slippages/errors/mistakes\] between people and machines \[@OUTLINE\_OF\_PAPER\_TO\_ACCOMPANY\_ELIZA\]. He continually returned focus to questions of (the impossibility of) human understanding rather than computational understanding: "the very asking of the question, 'Has the computer captured the essence of man?' is a diversion and, in that sense, a trap. For the real question, 'Does man understand the essence of man?' cannot be answered by any technological instrument" \[@weizenbaumImpactComputerSociety1972, p.611\]. Don't assume computer 'understanding' is happening, or that it is a useful or achievable goal. Even during person-to-person communication, we continue to converse productively despite knowing we will never arrive at full mutual understanding.

Lack of machine understanding is not a barrier to human-computer communication, and neither should machine understanding be our aim. Perhaps we can hold computational agents to a different standard. Rather than task them with unquantifiable goals like intelligence, might we embrace Weizenbaum's insistence that bots do not know. Instead, we can expect from them and their makers only partial theories — knowing as we do their structures and their limitations, and our own. Rather than trying to optimize for understanding, we can reach for something partial, something lighter. We can interact with automated systems and not expect their agency, but instead use them to learn more about what we don't know. As Weizenbaum says, "Indeed, one of the most cogent reasons for using computers is to expose holes in our thinking. Computers are merciless critics in this respect" \[@weizenbaumComputerPowerHuman1976, p. 65\]. Might we imagine computational perspectives that are entirely different from our perspective, rather than trying to mimic what we already think we know?

## **Relate Differently**

Weizenbaum resisted anthropomorphizing computer systems and warned about their illusory charms. In building ELIZA to be adaptable to other scripts, he also mapped the potential for software tools not beholden to any single imaginary. In the SPACKS script, ELIZA parses a poem called "The Freshmen" by Barry Spacks. The resulting dialogue unpacks the meaning of a line referencing a WWII German concentration camp: "Is any heart in order after Belsen?" Consider if ELIZA were not known solely as a feminized therapy bot, but as a "translating processor," or as a math teacher, or a poet, or as a multifaceted tool that was all of these. If ELIZA had been known by its other scripts, imagine its legacies in those alternate futures. What alternate scripts and alternate futures can we imagine for AI systems now? What would you wish a bot to do if someone had not already told you what it should do? Imagine what causal models, and computation in general, could offer if we were not fixed into the models we have been given that focus on imitating intelligence[^12] in one particular optimized, machine-readable representation. ELIZA offers new potential scripts for human-computer relationality, beyond a reductive focus on mimicry, analogy, parody, categorization, and stereotype by default.

Causal language models remain less predictable than rule-based systems like ELIZA, but their unpredictability does not mean they are uninterpretable or unimpeachable. In their scale and complexity, it is easy to forget that they require just as much human decision-making, influence, and control. Weizenbaum insists that we can "deny technology the prerogative to formulate man's questions" \[@weizenbaumImpactComputerSociety1972\]. Computer systems are not neutral. We build them and give them direction. When we say, "Please, go on..." we must also ask, "Where do we want to go? Where do we want to avoid?" Those questions have consequences for us all.

Joseph Weizenbaum did not intend for ELIZA to be a chatbot sensation, just as large language models (LLMs) did not begin as chatbots. In each case, paradigms have shifted when the chatbot "enters the chat." The use of artificial agent interfaces facilitated the pivot from their tech industry niche awareness into household names, creating widespread misunderstandings in their wake. This pivot echos the gap between cultural understandings of AI hype and the material sociotechnical processes that support actual AI systems more broadly. In particular, a divide persists between how technologists understand and use language models and how the non-technical public understand and use them.
