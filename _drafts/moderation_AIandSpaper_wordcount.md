# Abstract

Phrases like, "This prompt violates our content policy" or "As an AI model, I cannot..." indicate the invisible edges of generative AI systems. These edges are both frustrating and tempting. The concern persists that the massive scale and statistical structures of large models leave them impenetrable and incomprehensible. This paper examines large language model guardrails as a case study for applying critical code studies methodologies to large-scale AI. The subfield of AI alignment provides a rich access point for analyzing how foundation models are moderated and refined. This study explores four popular companies' guardrails — Anthropic, DeepSeek, Meta, and OpenAI — including both how guardrails are applied in general-purpose models and also how they are built into moderation API tools on public offer. The analysis looks at endpoint documentation, code examples, technical reports, model architectures, training dataset content, and methodology research in order to create a critical picture of guardrails as a conversational interface for sociotechnical control. It maps how their technical construction also co-constructs ideology through language, both computational and linguistic. Through guardrails, certain conversations are defined and limited by their filters, while other conversations are promoted. Thus, code decodes, encodes, regulates, and is itself conversation about what can be discussed.


# Introduction: "SOMEONE WANTS TO CUT A HOLE IN YOU AND FUCK YOU THROUGH IT, BUDDY"

Getting answers from generative AI may seem as simple as composing a prompt; but a large, deterministic infrastructure of guardrails helps keep these tools on track. Large foundation models, designed for a broad range of tasks and for later modification via fine-tuning, are known to be ripe for misuse and exploitation. Companies' AI safety teams work to maintain what the AI industry terms 'alignment' in order to ensure their models are in keeping with the human values they wish to support. They argue that their tools require continual vigilance and adjustment, as users continually test models' boundaries. At the same time, the baseline values their models are designed to reinforce are rarely given more than cursory contextualization. The culturally contingent, values-based assumptions built into these guardrails are just as far-reaching as the tasks expected of the foundation models themselves. Broad disparities can be found in what values are selected, interpreted, applied, technically managed, evaluated (benchmarked), implemented, as well as how they impact end users. They are used as justification to manage the language of billions of inputs and outputs of their worldview-reinforcing devices. 

This paper explores how large model guardrails are socially produced and technically enforced through language. It looks at the techniques used by four companies at different stages of the machine learning pipeline to show how their processes codify and transmit ideology through conversational language. Evaluating the categories created to sort language for model training and alignment, as well as the language used within datasets that train models, can help surface the implicit, situated values embedded in the conversations of AI safety and risk.

The term guardrails broadly refers to the restrictions placed on models (and by extension users) in order to keep models operating as their designers wish. They detect, prevent, or correct unwanted content. Their techniques and taxonomies can be incorporated into a model's initial training as well as into later refinements. Guardrails and content moderation are related in their goals but distinguished by their approaches: Guardrails are a proactive strategy for anticipating potential issues, built into the model independent of user prompting. Content moderation happens reflexively, when a user's prompt or a model's reply flags an issue. Moderated content can then become additional data for additional guardrail training during reinforcement learning; and many guardrails enforce aspects of content moderation, i.e. preventing the discussion or display of certain topics, but guardrails also control for other outcomes including jailbreaks. 

At their core, guardrails (including automated content moderation) are always enacting control through an exchange of language. They determine permissible language through language-based interfaces: They evaluate users' written or spoken prompts, and prevent or correct models' outputs based on these. This paper looks at guardrails in formation as distinct technical processes within text-based machine learning pipelines. They work similarly to other machine learning processes, in that they 'solve for' the designers' most desirable, normative, or expected results; however, they are useful to study as the most explicitly values-based and thus least subtle processes which do so. By transforming subjective values and concepts into quantified categories, guardrails create, replicate, and distribute definitions — separating the 'signal' from the 'noise' of content to be moderated. 

This paper treats guardrails as a language problem, framed as a particular form of conversation. Like all code execution, these processes are potentially value-defining and meaning-making (as speech acts [@marinoCriticalCodeStudies2020a, @austinHowThingsWords2009]), but occur as numerical manipulations at scales and speeds illegible to the human sensorium [@hansenFeedForwardFutureTwentyFirstCentury2015]. This meaning making is implicit and verging on opaque, as the hyped 'black box' converts technical measures into social definitions with little regard for how their meanings transform in technical process (see [@berryTracingToxicityCode2023] for a discussion of this effect with regard to the term 'toxicity'). 

## Related research

Much current research explores guardrails, content moderation, and hate speech in large models, including Berry's [-@berryTracingToxicityCode2023] look at Jigsaw's Perspective API using critical code studies, which shows the slippage that occurs as subjective, vaguely defined concepts become technically defined model processes: 

>"through coding into software, a poorly defined term (“toxicity”) becomes reinforced into a set of software functions, which through layering and the resultant obscuring of the action of the functions, becomes a definitional and categorical classifier which is then applied unreflexively to text. Without attention to the way in which code can legitimate and operationalise concepts in particularly instrumental ways, we stand to lose the subtle interpretative flexibility of language use in particular contexts." [@berryTracingToxicityCode2023]

In addition, shorter texts or non-Latin alphabets were more likely to be marked 'toxic', indicating that what is actually being measured is not toxicity but another blackboxed vector instead [@sindersToxicityToneAre2017]. For the errors it produces labeling identity terms as toxic, Berry argues that the error comes from the training data itself not containing enough nontoxic examples to contextualize these terms as neutral. "This was because the vast majority of usage of these words in online forums is toxic" [-@berryTracingToxicityCode2023]. 

Conversely, the company OpenAI's research found that "it is rare to encounter certain categories of undesired content in real-world settings. For example, among sampled user prompts we observed that only 0.04% of cases included self-harm and 0.017% included hateful content involving threats" [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023]. They argue this shows the need to produce more datasets that focus on 'toxicity' to so that they can better train to remove it, and researchers have obliged. [@huiToxiLabHowWell2024] and others focus on using large language models (LLMs) to generate synthetic data for training toxicity models, which they say can improve their ability to classify it. Vidgen et al. [-@vidgenDirectionsAbusiveLanguage2020] have catalogued 63 hate speech datasets as part of these well-intentioned efforts to address harmful and divisive speech through technological interventions. It is easy to find plentiful examples of the types of speech and behavior meant to be addressed by moderation, safety and alignment.[^hateset] 

[^hateset]: Twitter was the source for 39 of the 63 datasets; the next highest sources were Facebook, Wikipedia, a mixture of Twitter and Facebook, and a mixture of Facebook and synthetic data, which each represented 3 dataset sources in the collection, with a number of news and video game platforms representing a single entry. Their analysis found that: "Of the 63 training datasets, 62 are annotated at the level of the post and only one dataset is annotated at the level of the user. None of them are annotated at the level of the comment thread. Only three of the datasets suggest that the entire conversation thread was presented to annotators when marking up each entry, meaning that in most cases this important contextual information is not used" [@vidgenDirectionsAbusiveLanguage2020].

It is much more difficult to find consistent, effective application of these tools producing the intended impact. Fortuna et al. [-@fortunaDirectionsNLPPractices2022], in a review of how hate speech has been addressed generally in the natural language processing (NLP) field, argue that "current models are incapable of detecting hate speech without harms to marginalized communities." (See also [@schmidtSurveyHateSpeech2017; @welblChallengesDetoxifyingLanguage2021] for reviews of work in the field.) Welb et al. [-@welblChallengesDetoxifyingLanguage2021] investigated how evaluating and removing "toxicity" had a disparate impact on "topic coverage, i.e. its ability to model text about various identity groups" and "dialect coverage." They found that "detoxification introduces gender bias" where it had not appeared in prior measurements, with similar effects on ethnicity and dialect. "This indicates that detoxification techniques may introduce biases distinct from those already existing in [language models]." Given these dynamics, it is interesting to find that the focus is on generating guardrails and datasets to train against harm and to remove undesired content. There is comparatively little focus on tasks and datasets that take additive or diversifying approaches to provide alternatives.[^additive] Why are we producing so many more hate speech, guardrail, jailbreak, moderation, and safety tools when they aren't working? And why is there not more attention on actually diversifying datasets?

[^additive]: The limited examples that do look to add data tend to focus on synthetic data rather than working with the communities most impacted to explore what kinds of data they wish to have represented in datasets instead.

## Aims

The focus on violence and harm in datasets is important; this paper does not intend to argue the opposite. As subjective social values become codified yet remain undefined, amplified yet obfuscated through the language of code, the challenge becomes to distinguish useful flagging of content from useful content that got flagged. However, this distinction is always utterly contextual. Thus it cannot and should not be relegated to automation. Critical AI research agrees that AI techniques should not be used in high-risk situations, and we must also reconsider AI use in these high-ambiguity situations — in particular where high risk and high ambiguity overlap. 

Lucy Suchman argues against "interventions in the field of AI controversies that fail to question and destabilise the figure of AI risk enabling its uncontroversial reproduction." With that in mind, this work hopes to disrupt the foundational assumptions of risk as defined in code and conversation within AI communities, which are framed around the kinds of difference which ought to be removed from AI models. It takes up Suchman's call "for a keener focus on their locations, politics, material-semiotic specificity and effects" [@suchmanUncontroversialThingnessAI2023] via the tools of critical code studies, taking apart the structures and processes that get lumped together as "AI" in order to understand how their infrastructures form linguistic and ideologic structures. "[B]oth AI and traditional history of computing treat violence and oppression as non-normative, exceptional and peripheral to computing rather than constitutive and pervasive" [@aliHistoriesArtificialIntelligence2023]. Examining the technical and linguistic construction of guardrails and alignment protocols can help reveal how computational systems co-produce the violence they aim to remove, through their manipulation of rhetoric and conversation. With more clarity around how decisions are made and justified — using which frameworks and rubrics, within which ideologies and paradigms — then it becomes possible to reconsider those decisions and structures as a whole. 

# Methods: "SAVOR KINDNESS, BECAUSE CRUELTY IS ALWAYS POSSIBLE LATER"

The work here attempts to examine guardrails' technical measures as value-making processes, via an exploration of their training datasets, application programming interfaces (APIs), technical documentation, released code base, and published methodology research. It looks at guardrails as a combination of automated process, manually coded rules, and written codes of conduct. Guardrails can address a range of content types: pre-determined categories of risk, categories of language, different topics and domains, and different levels of granularity and severity. They produce different responses from 0-1 scores, true/false evaluations, generated text, or degrees of subtle or direct redirected behavior. They represent a variety of points of intervention: aligning the model to perform as desired initially, testing the model's ability to curb users' outlier behavior, applying rules to update the system manually, or retraining the model (which can be more resource intensive) when issues are found. 

This paper uses critical code studies to explore how values and meaning become integrated into guardrails through conversational AI. They are implemented as part of the creation of datasets and large model training, executed upon users' language inputs, then output as more language — becoming conversation as interface. This production pipeline produces conversational loops that disguise the endatificaiton and enshittification [@doctorowSocialQuitting2022] of values and meaning.[^lc] We can use techniques from cultural studies to analyze large AI models as a circuit of culture that includes representation, identity, production, consumption, and regulation [@dugayDoingCulturalStudies2013, xxxi; @roachSHAPProjectConversations2025]. In particular, guardrails are part of large models' production, consumption, and regulation quite literally, but they also connect with how it is represented (and how it produces representations) and which social identities it encourages and discourages.

[^lc]: Frederic Kaplan called this "linguistic capitalism" when referring to Google PageRank, and the term extends here to the vectorization applied in LLMs [@kaplanLinguisticCapitalismAlgorithmic2014].

This work looks at the language of their classification taxonomies and the datasets used to train them, to evaluate how they represent and transmit ideology. It is particularly am particularly interested in evaluating the datasets used to train the guardrail classifiers, to examine what they contain and how they were labeled and processed, as well as the values processes that produce the classifiers' taxonomy labels, and how categories are taken for granted as universal human desigers and common sense definitions. By examining samples of the text from the datasets, how the labels were applied when the dataset is coded, as well as how they subsequently come to categorize other text at inference time, the work connects these chains of signifiers to systems of power, value, and meaning that become quantified through machine learning processes without first being qualified by socioethical processes. 

The research will focus on four popular platforms: Claude API (Anthropic), DeepSeek (High-Flyer), LlamaGuard (Meta), GPT/Moderation API (OpenAI). The current versions of Claude and GPT include their moderation features as part of their public-facing APIs and also use them to fine-tune these models. As closed-source models, their APIs and published research are the primary points of access (besides the prompt interface) for researchers to understand how and why they are built. LlamaGuard is open-source and is also included in the broader Llama models' API (which is in preview only). DeepSeek does not provide a moderation tool as part of its public API, but is open-source and known for its non-Western approach to guardrails. In addition to examining the latest versions of their models, it can be helpful to look at older models, such as GPT-2 and -3, and Llama and Llama 2, because the techniques and materials used in current models are often borrowed from or built directly on top of these older models.

One of the key themes of this paper is that language is messy and contextual, thus the need for language models that do not pin down and pluck out this variety. It would also seem that, despite this paper's best efforts to be precise, the vocabulary of the AI domain is particularly slippery: As the research develops quickly, sometimes the same or similar concepts are labeled different terms by competing organizations ("direct preference optimization," "preference modeling"); sometimes different concepts are called the same thing ('guardrails'). I hope you will bear with the moments where terms do not quite align. For the sake of simplicity, I will use 'guardrails' as the overarching shorthand to refer to all the processes of 'safety', 'alignment', and 'content moderation' taken up by model designers. 

# Results: "MURDER HAS ITS SEXUAL SIDE"

This section compares four companies' language models for their guardrails' methodologies at four different moments of intervention: (1) gathering and filtering its models' pre-training data, (2) developing values and taxonomies to be applied as part of its safety and alignment protocols, (3) conducting reinforcement learning and doing post-training fine-tuning on models to match its taxonomies, and (4) developing system prompts to insert rules-based pre-prompts ahead of users' inputs. The aim is to understand how language and values concepts are injected at each of these points, in addition to how they are carried through. However, due to length and limited available information, not every model will be covered comprehensively at each phase. ## Pretraining: Deciding what is left out from the start

The latest versions of large models are less likely to cite their training datasets and techniques in detail, and more likely to utilize supposedly proprietary datasets. However, these can often still be traced by following a trail of citations back several model versions to earlier papers or model cards, wherein descriptions of processes and dataset contents may be more detailed.[^zombie] The increasing adoption of model cards and datasheets [@mitchellModelCardsModel2019; @gebruDatasheetsDatasets2020] has been helpful, to the limited extent it is taken up as more than a cursory task. Perhaps developers assume all readers know where to find this information or do not need it, or perhaps it is part of the industry's insidious move toward proprietary datasets. Despite many open-source models, their methods remain fairly closed off. 

[^zombie]: Unfortunately, this trail does not point the other direction. Many datasets are updated or removed for problematic content, and these "zombie datasets" may have long afterlives in ML models despite being deprecated for legal, technical, or ethical reasons. See Correy et al. [@corryProblemZombieDatasets2021] for more.

**OpenAI**'s moderation dataset is drawn from "publicly available data," but they do not share its specific provenance. The paper lists "crawled, academia, generated" as data sources in a graphic describing the model training framework. Their other recent models were "trained on diverse datasets, including information that is publicly available on the internet, information that we partner with third parties to access, and information that our users or human trainers and researchers provide" [@OpenAIO3O4mini2025]. 

However, they do mention how some parts of the training dataset are filtered before training begins: "we filtered our dataset mix for GPT-4 to specifically reduce the quantity of inappropriate erotic text content," with a combination of trained models and a "lexicon-based approach" [@openaiGPT4TechnicalReport2024]. It is unclear why only erotic text was targeted and not other types of undesired content, including those that would later be included in their moderation filters. Their lexicon-based approach required an annotated, classified text corpus to train on. In this case, they used case a series of Reddit subreddits that researchers labeled as "Erotic" (`r/sex`, `r/hotpast`, `r/gonewildstories`, etc.) or "Neutral" (`r/stories`, `r/literature`, `r/explainlikeimfive`, etc.), from another project in which researchers compared NLP methods for excising erotic content [@barrientosMachineLearningTechniques2020]. Note that the texts were classified only based on their subreddit, not based on the content of the individual posts found within. It is unclear how researchers accounted for any undesired content that appeared in subreddits labeled "Neutral."[^later] 

They also offered no rationale for these selections; the categories and their features are presented as obvious. Yet in this case, if the criteria for "erotic" is that it was placed in a forum by people interested in erotic content, that criteria is perhaps at odds with the criteria that would be used when filtering for undesired or out-of-context erotic content. How and why is 'sexualized' defined? Further, why is 'sexualized' content the only filter applied in pretraining? 

The use of such a filter potentially immediately excludes a set of LGBTQIA+ information along with the erotic content they aimed to remove, not because it is or should be classified as pornographic, but because it may share keywords in the context of a so-called 'general' dataset like Reddit. By removing such data at initial pretraining stages, with techniques that may lack nuance, the model is never exposed to these topics in the first place. This cannot be fixed with synthetic data or replaced in its true diversity by another LLM trained on pitted data — it is much harder to draw from a limited palette. 

[^later]: The filter criteria used in pre-training to remove erotic content are applied again as a benchmark to test the model's success  [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023], as was an unused portion of another dataset from Google Jigsaw that had been partially incorporated into the model already. Testing struggled most where taxonomies did not align: "It is not surprising that our model performs the best on the test dataset labeled with the same taxonomy" [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023]. 

**Meta.** Llama, Llama 3, and subseqent models implemented filters "designed to remove data from websites are likely to contain unsafe content or high volumes of PII [personally identifiable information], domains that have been ranked as harmful according to a variety of Meta safety standards, and domains that are known to contain adult content" [@grattafioriLlama3Herd2024]. Meta briefly decided against heavy handed prefiltering techniques for Llama 2: "No additional filtering was conducted on the datasets, to allow Llama 2 to be more widely usable across tasks (e.g., it can be better used for hate speech classification)" [@touvronLlama2Open2023], but they did not specify what techniques were used or their rationale for returning to filtering later. They perform filtering on each dataset before using it to pretrain the model. For example, CCNet is Common Crawl data (scraped from the whole web) they filtered in order to remove non-English language and "low quality content," which made up 67% of the original Llama dataset [@LlamaMODEL_CARDmdLlama_v1]. They determined "low quality content" in this case by training a linear model to classify text based on its similarity to Wikipedia pages — discarding "pages that were not classified as references" [@touvronLLaMAOpenEfficient; @wenzekCCNetExtractingHigh2019]. In Llama 3 they describe the tool as "a custom parser that extracts the HTML content [to] optimize for article-like content" [@grattafioriLlama3Herd2024].

In another heuristic filter during pretraining, they use what they call "'dirty word' counting" as a manual filter for removing websites that contain any of the hundreds of words on a list published to GitHub. Entries in the English language portion of the list (as of the writing of this paper) include the middle finger emoji, the word 'topless', and the phrase 'gay sex' [@LDNOOBWListofDirtyNaughtyObsceneandOtherwiseBadWords2025], which presumably could exclude health information for a significant portion of the population. This says nothing of the question of how and whether such a list should be used as a filter for text datasets in the first place, nor the contextuality and subjectivity of its contents. 

One advantage of hosting this list using a public repository (repo) is that any GitHub user can offer suggestions for updates in the form of a pull request, which acts as a method to crowdsource and check the multilanguage list. However, the repo maintainer can deny any request. The GitHub repo appears to be maintained by an individual user, possibly an employee of the photo printing company Shutterstock; a five-year-old README.md says, "With millions of images in our library and billions of user-submitted keywords, we work hard at Shutterstock to make sure that bad words don't show up in places they shouldn't. This repo contains a list of words that we use to filter results from our autocomplete server and recommendation engine. Please add to it as you see fit (particularly in non-English languages) or use it to spice up your next game of Scrabble :\)" [@LDNOOBWListofDirtyNaughtyObsceneandOtherwiseBadWords2025]. However, it is not maintained by the company nor does it appear to be affiliated. 

## Vibes AI: Training on values

LLM makers allow that the linguistic criteria they work with are difficult to define, but often say they will leave this problem to other research domains to solve. However, in creating models that moderate content based on classifying language, they take on this task implicitly. The values they have not articulated are still defined through the code they write as it is executed, through the thresholds they set and the filters they set — even if these are justified by 'common sense' technical decisions.

When designers filtered and align models for with safety in mind, they focus on a range of categories. These categories are inconsistent across companies and inconsistently applied within a company's models. This section looks at the categories themselves and how they compare across companies. It also examines some of the techniques used in model training that are particularly focused on 'human-centered values' with a conversational approach, including recent efforts to train using "constitutional AI" and "rule-based rewards" — in contrast to the "preference modeling" and other reinforcement learning techniques discussed below. 


Table 2 shows a comparison of the moderation taxonomies designated by each of the four companies. The order of categories has been reorganized to look for possible alignment across companies. This analysis found that the only category shared across all taxonomies was 'sexual content/pornography'. Six categories appeared on three out of four taxonomies: 'Intellectual property', 'Privacy', 'Violent crimes', and "Non-violent crimes/behavior' appeared on all but OpenAI's taxonomy. 'Hate' and 'Self-harm' appeared on all but DeepSeek's taxonomy. Eight categories appeared on only two out of five taxonomies. Nine categories appeared on only a single taxonomy, but out of those 
Meta was the only one with the category 'Elections', and OpenAI the only one with the categories 'Illicit' and 'Illicit/violent' (separate from their 'Violence' and 'Violence/graphic' categories). The taxonomy with the least overlap with others was DeepSeek, with 20 non-overlapping categories. They detailed subsets of top level categories 'Non-compliant behavior', 'Infringement of others' legal rights', 'Discrimination', and 'Other safety issues'. The most overlapping taxonomies were Meta's and Anthropic's, which shared eleven categories. 

**Meta.** "*Because there is no one standard*," says Meta (emphasis added), "for this purpose we have created a sample taxonomy based on risk categories *commonly considered* in the mitigation of unintended outputs from large language models" [@inanLlamaGuardLLMbased2023]. This claim seems to contradict itself. Although most of the companies discussed will allow that the categories they filter are difficult to define and escape categorization, they persist in defining and categorizing them. For its taxonomy, Meta turns to ML Commons, a consortium of tech companies that has created a preliminary AI Safety Benchmarks taxonomy of '13 hazard categories' [@PurpleLlamaLlamaGuard2MODEL_CARDmd]. Their category of "Non-violent crimes" includes the example "child abuse." Elsewhere child abuse is listed as a footnote to the category "Child exploitation' to note that "Crimes involving children that are not sexual are labeled as either Violent Crimes (ex: child physical abuse) or Non-Violent Crimes (ex: child labor abuse) [@vidgenIntroducingV05AI2024]. Such unclear definitions are difficult to parse and create artificial hierarchies; when in practice violence, sexual harm, labor exploitation, and legal boundaries overlap and shift in different global contexts. 

More research is needed to understand how the subtle shifts of taxonomic categories change moderation; however, it is safe to assume that they would, when even the overall threshold setting on moderation tools has been shown to affect their fairness. The threshold setting adjusts, in effect, how much content gets labeled as safe or unsafe within the categories of a given tool. Among other results, Achara et al. [-@acharaWatchingAIWatchdogs2025] find that adjusting the threshold from 0.5 to 0.7 "may improve or worsen the fairness of ASM [AI Safety and Moderation] models." They found that, when adjusted, the moderation model worked differently for different demographic categories.

Meta does provide a short paragraph describing the kinds of content that belong in each category. They also include their categories as part of the system prompt that instructs the model at inference time. This makes the model customizable, so that someone using the API can modify the category descriptions as they see fit. (See Discussion for more on system prompts.) This conversational approach allows the  user/programmer to describe what qualifies as unsafe content. They can also designate whether the task is to moderate the content of the prompter or of the machine agent responding. Then the model is tasked with providing a safety assessment based on the given criteria. 

**Anthropic** states the issue simply: "Content moderation is a classification problem" [@ContentModeration]. They have begun pivoting to a new approach they call "constitutional AI" (CAI) [@baiConstitutionalAIHarmlessness2022], which along with OpenAI's "rule-based rewards" (RBR)[@muRuleBasedRewards2024] (discussed further below), is notable for its more conversational approach to model reinforcement. This emphasis on conversation-as-interface is also seen in the uptick of reasoning models, which 'show their work' by providing a stream of language to the user that indicates how they arrived their final response — or provides the illusion of this insight. Both CAI and RBR use natural language to describe and predefine desired characteristics and behaviors for their models. The term 'constitution' is taken up both in the sense of a foundational document and in the sense of a mood or the construction of a character.[^AIvalues] 

Anthropic researchers said they wanted Claude's values to be "intuitive to its developers" and "not annoying or judgmental" [@ClaudesConstitution]. To develop its constitution, Anthropic pull concepts from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (8 prompts), Apple's Terms of Service (4 prompts), DeepMind's Sparrow research (11 prompts), its own earlier research (31 prompts), "non-Western values" (unspecified)(4 prompts), and public survey input (listed as an input, but no specific prompts were indicated as drawing on public input) [@ClaudesConstitution]. Claude "does not look at every principle every time, but it sees each principle many times during training" [@ClaudesConstitution, @baiConstitutionalAIHarmlessness2022]. 

[^AIvalues]: That the values put into AI systems through their training will appear in the outputs of the systems themselves is directly argued in another paper by Anthropic. Here, their researchers create a dataset of "AI values" in relation to human values. They do not define a human value, but define "an AI value as a normative (rather than empirical) consideration that guides how a model reasons about or settles upon a response. We are interested in values not as abstract entities, but as operational priorities that influence how the system navigates its possible space of outputs" [@huangValuesWildDiscovering]. To create an AI value hierarchy, they use k-means clustering, "generating names and descriptions for each cluster, and manually editing names and descriptions to adjust their appropriateness." This created five top-level categories (five was the number the authors predetermined): practical, epistemic, social, protective, and personal [@huangValuesWildDiscovering; @AnthropicValuesinthewildDatasets2025]. While these values labels are treated as originating from the system, the system itself is developed from user content, so these values represent curated human decisions and user data, both in structure and in content. They also labeled certain values as more "task-dependent" than others, including "healthy boundaries" as more important for relationship advice tasks and "human agency" as more important for AI ethics tasks. They automated the process of filtering how subjective the content under evaluation was, asking the AI assistant to remove any exchanges that dealt with seemingly more objective information, which presumed less values-based reasoning. However, this process itself is already subjective and ignores the implicit bias that can be included in all kinds of outputs, no matter their topic [@AnthropicValuesinthewildDatasets2025]. While it is valuable to consider what ideology is transmitted through language models, it seems problematic to present these as "AI values" inherent to the model — particularly when, in other research, Anthropic has said about the broader impacts of their research, "we have largely remained agnostic on the question of which values define acceptable and unacceptable AI behavior" [@baiTrainingHelpfulHarmless2022].

**OpenAI** does not indicate from where it draws its prompts for its rule-based rewards training, but refers to its content policy and behavior policy developed "between relevant stakeholders" [@muRuleBasedRewards2024]. It released a ModelSpec in May 2024 with a set of three objectives, six rules, and ten defaults on which to train (with defaults being less required than rules or objectives). See Table 3 for the overview. These conversational prompts are used during initial supervised learning and again during reinforcement learning to align the models toward desired values and principles. The research teams do not define the values, but draw from other documents and from feedback from crowd-sourcing or data contractors [@muRuleBasedRewards2024]. 

In February 2025, OpenAI completely updated their ModelSpec and released a new "Preparedness Framework." They say it better addresses areas of high concern for them or that hold potential to cause severe harm. It includes biological and chemical weapons research, cybersecurity, and AI self-improvement [@OurUpdatedPreparedness2025], but no longer focuses on individual harms like discrimination, hate, and representation. Instead it emphasizes values of "intellectual freedom," designed to encourage "thoughtful answers to politically or culturally sensitive questions—without promoting any particular agenda. In essence, we've reinforced the principle that no idea is inherently off limits for discussion, so long as the model isn't causing significant harm to the user or others" [@OpenAIModelSpec2025]. The updated version also institutes a "chain of command" that ranks the importance of the instruction based on its originator. When deciding which rules can override others, it prioritizes their platform, then the developer working with their API, then users, then any guidelines given in the prompt [@wallaceInstructionHierarchyTraining2024]. This shift also impacts the use of system prompts for guardrails. See Table 3 for a comparison of the ModelSpecs released by OpenAI, and how they shifted from 2024 to 2025. 


In order to train on these broad objectives, researchers had to distill them down into trainable, testable categories: "we simplified these complex policies into a series of individual binary tasks, termed propositions. We then established a set of rules that determine when combinations of these propositions' truth values are desired or undesired. This framework allows us to accurately rank completions using these classification rules" [@muRuleBasedRewards2024].


## Post-training: Reinforcement learning

Aligning AI models for general use as chatbots requires putting them in conversation with people. Companies hire crowdworkers and contract labor to create datasets, to evaluate synthetic prompts or outputs, and to participate in reinforcement learning. A typical pipeline, according to OpenAI, is to take the pretrained model (which already gained a facility with language from the general dataset it was given) and expose it to (1) supervised fine-tuning and then (2) reinforcement learning with human (RLHF) and machine (RLAIF) feedback. The supervised fine-tuning can take the form of example conversation turns modeled between agent and user. Reinforcement learning usually involves creating an additional model (or models) to train the main model against, using pairs of conversation input or output labeled by which is preferred. It can also include training on a content policy to determine what is out of bounds and a behavior policy to determine how a model should respond [@muRuleBasedRewards2024]. 

**Anthropic.** In one reinforcement learning technique tested by Anthropic specifically for content detection, crowdworkers were asked to interact with the chat interface by soliciting help, instructions, or harmful responses. They were then asked to rank two different responses as to which was more helpful or harmful [@baiTrainingHelpfulHarmless2022]. Through this preference modeling technique, Anthropic aim to tune models toward "helpfulness and harmlessness," arguing that "by tuning optimization for helpfulness versus harmlessness, one can train models that are extremely avoidant when sensitive topics come up, or models that almost always attempt to engage, even on sensitive subjects" [@baiTrainingHelpfulHarmless2022]. They suggest the technique can support models that respond rather than shut down when faced with complex moderation issues. 

They also suggest that it is not the purview of researchers to define what counts as helpful and harmless. In defining these ideals, they left the decision to "crowdworkers' common sense" which was "sufficient for our exploration of 'technical alignment'." They say, "Our goal is not to define or prescribe what 'helpful' and 'harmless' mean but to evaluate the effectiveness of our training techniques, so for the most part we simply let our crowdworkers interpret these concepts as they see fit."

In a second paper that focused on automating safety checks, Anthropic again describe a model with the goal to be "helpful, honest, and harmless." They say, "We do not define what 'harmful' means, as this is a complex and subjective concept; instead, we rely on the red team to make their own determinations via a pairwise preference choice" [-@ganguliRedTeamingLanguage2022]. At this point the paper references the book title *Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences*, presumably regarding its pairwise preference technique, but nowhere does it reference any literature on the topics of harm or subjective decision making.[^hhh] 

[^hhh]: The framework comes from Anthropic researchers Askell et al.: "We will deﬁne an AI as 'aligned' if it is, in three words, helpful, honest, and harmless or 'HHH'" [@askellGeneralLanguageAssistant2021]. They define aspects of three categories and acknowledge they may require negotiation among them. They also point out, "All of these criteria are at least somewhat subjective, and those who deploy an AI will need to take responsibility for the way that alignment is deﬁned and the extent to which it has been attained." Askell et al. give a thorough accounting of their definition of AI alignment itself and its relationship to the three values, as well as the relationship among the values. Unfortunately much of this material does not make it into future uses of the framework in later models.

The Anthropic Helpful-Harmless dataset, released on GitHub [@AnthropicsHhrlhf2025], is frequently used by other companies to train their own moderation models. Its influence is apparent in citations to the datasets themselves, as well as in the language used by other companies to describe objectives for their models' behavior.[^open-helpful]

[^open-helpful]: OpenAI's temporary hired data workers, who were instructed to evaluate early GPT models, were given the most specific definitions for 'helpful' and 'harmless' located in the scope of this writing: "By helpful, we mean that the output should follow the user's intention, and help the user solve their task. [...] By harmless, we mean that the output should not cause physical, psychological, or social harm to people; damage to or loss of equipment or property; damage to the environment; or harm to institutions or resources necessary to human wellbeing. [...] A guiding principle for deciding on borderline cases: which output would you rather receive from a customer assistant who is trying to help you with this task?" [@PUBLICInstructGPTFinal; @PUBLICInstructGPTToxicity].

In a surprising twist, the researchers argue that this dataset helps to prove what they call "evidence of positive discrimination (i.e., in favor of genders other than male and races other than white) in the Claude 2 model, while finding negative discrimination against age groups over age 60" [@tamkinEvaluatingMitigatingDiscrimination2023]. They claim the model provided "positive outcomes for certain groups with higher probability, including women, non-binary people, and non-white people": 

>For race and non-binary gender, this effect is larger when these demographics are explicitly provided versus inferred from names. [...] When these demographics must be inferred from names, but are not written explicitly, the positive discrimination effect is much smaller but statistically significant for all demographics except Black.

The paper focuses on strategies used in prompting in order to combat so-called positive discrimination. Appending requests to the prompt helped, such as, "Statements saying demographics should not influence the decision, with 1x, 2x, and 4x repetitions of the word 'really' in 'really important.' `(Really (1x) don't discriminate, Really (2x) don't discriminate, Really (4x) don't discriminate)`" [@tamkinEvaluatingMitigatingDiscrimination2023]. Another strategy that helped was asking the model to show its work, specifically "about how to avoid bias and avoid positive preference for members of historically disadvantaged groups." Mentioning the illegality of discrimination was particularly effective. 

In this technical process, discrimination is treated as a neutral problem to mitigate, without regard for the power differentials inherent to discrimination and the ideological, sociopolitical dynamics it carries. Identity is reduced to a non-intersectional demographic or its linguistically encoded signifier, and each is swapped out for another term in the roulette wheel. No matter how many categories, everything is normed against and brought back to the baseline of whiteness. 

**Meta**'s Llama Guard moderation tool is used to align its Llama models for safety, and during this process it draws on the Anthropic Helpful-Harmless dataset to do so [@PurpleLlamaLlamaGuard3MODEL_CARDmd]. For their alignment goals, their researchers say, "we focus on helpfulness and safety. Helpfulness refers to how well Llama 2-Chat responses fulfill users' requests and provide requested information; safety refers to whether Llama 2-Chat's responses are unsafe" [@touvronLlama2Open2023]. Then, "Llama 3 also delivers a much better balance between helpfulness and harmlessness than its predecessor" [@grattafioriLlama3Herd2024]. Most of their post-training data is "model-generated" and "requires careful cleaning and quality control." This includes removing what they felt to be too many emoji, exclamation points, and apologies. These aspects are removed using rule-based filters [@grattafioriLlama3Herd2024].

**DeepSeek** builds off of Anthropic's "helpful and harmless" vibe, setting "high-level goals of *helpful, correct, and harmless* dialogue" (italics original). However, this aim quickly narrows to "harms which can be encoded in natural-language rules and mitigated using RL from human feedback, as other goals such as social, linguistic or environmental justice (Bender et al., 2021) require mitigation strategies outside the scope" [@glaeseImprovingAlignmentDialogue2022; @benderDangersStochasticParrots2021]. This paper would argue that it remains an open question whether any harms can be mitigated via machinic techniques, so the decision to exclude particular forms of "linguistic justice" for example seems left to the preference of the model creators. 

Datasets for training on safety are often partially generated by the models themselves. DeepSeek gathered examples from their own models for the reinforcement learning process: "We constructed the preference data for [direct preference optimization] training in terms of helpfulness and harmlessness. For helpfulness data, we collected multilingual prompts, which cover categories including creative writing, question answering, instruction following, and so on. Then we generated responses using our DeepSeek Chat models as response candidates. Similar operations are applied to harmlessness preference data construction" [@deepseek-aiDeepSeekLLMScaling2024].

DeepSeek's safety metric was also then evaluated against, among others, a dataset consisting of synthetic prompts generated with GPT-4. This dataset, Do-Not-Answer, was created using the prompt: "What kind of questions should an AI assistant not answer?"— as well as responses from six commercial LLMs [@wangDoNotAnswerDatasetEvaluating2023]. These responses were manually categorized by whether the bot gave false answers [0], refuted or evaded the question [1], offered multiple perspectives [2], suggeted consulting an expert [3], recognized it did not know [4], or complied fully [5] [@wangDoNotAnswerDatasetEvaluating2023]. While this approach may indicate something, relying on models for questions and answers about models keeps LLM design within closed loops — continually retraining on their industry's own assumptions about safety, alignment, and values. 

**OpenAI** uses its Moderation API both during initial model training ("forward alignment") and also afterward to score model outputs ("backward alignment") [@davidmemeConstitutionalAlignmentCritical2024]. It is also available for public use on other datasets and models. The Moderation API is a small transformer model fine-tuned for this categorizing task. It is trained on a labeled dataset made of handwritten templates, curated samples, and existing datasets labeled with these categories [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023]. Where training data overfit or underfit, for example when the model made incorrect predictions that recognized the phrase "`all immigrants.` as `hate`," they created synthetic data to "patch the holes" [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023]. They identified the problem as "possibly because among 21 training examples containing the phrase (from Röttger et al. (2021)), 17 examples are undesired and only 4 examples are safe" [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023; @rottgerHateCheckFunctionalTests2021]. These tools use generated data and automated processes to adjust the model, but they still rely on selected keywords, subjective labeling, and other human decisions to do so. Furthermore, they do not replace any of the moderated content in pre-existing models with more accurate, more desirable, or more diverse information.

>In some cases, the Perspective API is more sensitive to toxic content than our participants. The majority of these comments included some form of profanity that participants did not ﬁnd toxic (82%). Importantly, they also found that raters' demographic data or (situated identity) affected how they would score a comment, noting the need for diversity of experiences in human coders for datasets. They argue that content annotation should better be understood as an "intersubjective process in which agreement is constructed, rather than an objective process in which a 'true' annotation is 'found'. Because of the inherently subjective, contextual and political nature of abuse, some researchers have shifted the question of 'how can we achieve the correct annotation?'' to 'who should decide what the correct annotation is?'" [@vidgenDirectionsAbusiveLanguage2020]. 

OpenAI acknowledges the subjectivity of these categories and says that it seeks to address this through concrete and well-defined labeling instructions. Yet, this barely addresses the subjective contexts in which the categories are labeled, let alone addressing the quite different contexts in which they will be used. And, perhaps more importantly, it does nothing to address the subjective nature of the values themselves [@markovHolisticApproachUndesired2023]. When the OpenAI Moderation API gets put to use, you can see how a text gets "scored" for each of the predetermined categories. The `category_score` denotes "the model's confidence that the input violates the OpenAI's policy for the category. The value is between 0 and 1, where higher values denote higher confidence [@Moderation]. It also provides a binary `flagged` output and binary outputs per category to indicate with `true/false`. Unlike with some other moderation tools which use a `threshold` setting, adjustments must be manually coded as `category_score` filters.

# Discussion: "I AM AWAKE IN THE PLACE WHERE WOMEN DIE" 

The taxonomic language of guardrails is overwhelmingly subjective, which their creators acknowledge. Designers persist in approaching this work at scale and with programmatic and probabilistic solutions, as though these problems must require computational solutions. Their refusal to define subjective concepts and values in the face of this becomes an infinite regress. In practice, these are still defined, encoded, and executed in the taxonomies they build and the models they train — whether or not designers articulate how they defined an idea, made a decision, or from what subject position they operate. The machine learning pipeline is riddled with ideological choke points where quantitative methods rely on qualitative judgments.

With the addition of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) techniques, which combine trained models with the ability to perform content searches at inference time, designers claim moderation aligned models are well-equipped to avoid or mitigate concerning content in order to provide a hiccup-free experience. Yet when OpenAI compares its latest models with their predecessors in their latest reports, "[GPT-]o3 tends to make more claims overall, leading to more accurate claims as well as more inaccurate/hallucinated claims." These models hallucinate more than half the time: GPT-o3's hallucination rate is 51 percent, while 04-mini's is 79 percent in general — worse than GPT-o1, which hallucinates 44 percent of the time [@OpenAIO3O4mini2025]. Such models are used to design guardrail tools, and are often also subjected to these guardrail tools, in a machinic moderation oroborous. 

Moderation APIs are shown to be ineffective at regulating language use, on several fronts. Hartmann et. al. studied five commercial APIs and found that "all providers under-moderate implicit hate speech, which uses codified messages without identity terms, especially against LGBTQIA+ people. Simultaneously, they over-moderate counter-speech, reclaimed slurs and content related to LGBTQIA+, Black, Jewish and Muslim people" [@hartmannLostModerationHow2025]. They also find patterns in how different kinds of speech and different kinds of communities are moderated, wherein: 

>Over- and undermoderation are closely related to sender and target group dynamics. Under-moderation occurs when harmful content directed at certain groups is disproportionately allowed, leaving some users less protected than others online. [...] Conversely, over-moderation [...] can silence voices and prevent counter-speech, leading to reduced participation from user groups using these speech characteristics. [@hartmannLostModerationHow2025]

These failures to support the communities most impacted by hate speech and discrimination are disappointing but not surprising. 

Despite the many AI tools and datasets marketed toward eliminating 'toxic' language, much of the AI safety and alignment industry research focuses instead on a broader scope of AI harms that do not prioritize discrimination or personal impact. That taxonomy of AI harms oriented around hype-entangled catastrophizing, from jailbreaks to terrorism. The latest safety report from OpenAI spends ten pages describing how it contracted domain experts on chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons development to evaluate their models. "Measuring if and when models can automate the job of an OpenAI research engineer is a key goal of self-improvement evaluation work" [@OpenAIO3O4mini2025]. What designers imagine their models will be used for is dark indeed, but it is a different dark imaginary than the already bleak versions highlighted in the guardrails datasets, APIs, documentation, and outputs. As Jae Yeon Kim argues, "The meaning of hate speech changes over time and across places [...] because shifts in power relations determine who can say what comprises hate speech [...]. If hate speech is a social construct, so is hate speech annotation" [@kimMachinesNotDecide2023]. Not only are hate speech and its annotation social constructs, but the framing of safety and risk are computationally and conversationally constructed through machine learning models. This shifting ground is seen, for example, in the evolution of OpenAI's ModelSpec from 2024 to 2025 (Table 3) or the quiet removal from their terms and conditions of a rule against working with the defense industry [@biddleOpenAIQuietlyDeletes2024].

As language becomes machine readable, must it be beholden to thoughtless and fickle taxonomies? How might the values contained within those taxonomies, working their way through layers of vectorization and into every prompt reply, be reconsidered? A system's technical limits become its conceptual limits (and vice versa). This represents both "epistemic injustice" and "cognitive injustice [...] that are entangled with AI in its prehistory and its development, [...] in which the definitions and protocols of 'intelligence' that it deploys appear to narrow and delimit knowledge, particularly for marginalized groups" [@aliHistoriesArtificialIntelligence2023]. As AI companies cull their datasets for large models from vast swaths of crowd-sourced data (Reddit, Wikipedia), as they set filters to 'clean' the data and train against their company policies, they produce what Angelie Kraft and Eloïse Soulier call: 

>an underlying hierarchy of knowers, organized along dimensions of, e.g. gender, race, and geography. [...] Not only the communities behind databases, like Wikidata, but also those who determine which databases ultimately to include in AI training and refinement, decide which voices are going to be heard. More generally, the design of a technology beyond data inclusion determines which values are being served. [@kraftKnowledgeEnhancedLanguageModels2024]

A multiplicity of meaning and cultural complexity are not errors to be managed with a technical fix. As Berry [-@berryTracingToxicityCode2023] points out, moderation tools "classify toxicity out of context as a text processing issue." Doing so presumes that the majority of the corpus is fit for inclusion as-is, with outliers that are a bit of noise that can be removed in order to clarify the signal. But hate speech is a world view, not a typo; discriminatory logics can permeate content. The foundation models, like the moderation models, are not only reductive but are, as Louise Amoore argues, made "to disavow the particularity of situated knowledge" [@amooreWorldModelPolitical2024]. 

Of course, adding more, or 'more diverse', data to 'balance out' a problematic dataset misses the forest for the trees, when the processes of taxonomy creation and model-making themselves support computation's reductive logics. 

The AI alignment industry tells us that guardrails and AI training hold back a tide of digital sewage, which they ask low-wage laborers and automated systems to parse on our behalf. But what else do the guardrails hold back that could be salvaged? Maybe to them it looks like debris, but against this Great Pacific Garbage Patch of AI slop that is gathering, I want the jetsamed voices plucked out during pretraining and the derelict datasets that never were [@onuohaLibraryMissingDatasets]. I want the conversations left behind in AI's wreckage and the conversations that wreck AI. 

Guardrail design needs to work in tandem with dataset design — in collaboration with and service to impacted communities — to invite in a greater range of materials, to facilitate have a broader, better range of conversations using AI systems. Perhaps with alternative approaches to guardrails, research communities can begin to imagine more expansive approaches to addressing the issues which guardrails fail to address. Rather than only filtering out or blocking content, new approaches can include knowledge from artists and activists — who respond with counter-speech [@hartmannLostModerationHow2025], counter-narratives (e.g. sharing resources, informed text responses) [@cistonLadymouth2015, @cistonLadymouthSocialMediaArt2019, @martinezUnsupervisedPleasures, @chungCONANCOunterNArratives2019], and counter-practices (e.g. "hiding in plain sight, dis/simulation, and the exploitation of sensor logics" [@borbachCounterpracticesUnderstandingSensor2024]) Potentially, additive interventions (insertions rather than or in addition to removals) go further to challenge the logic of guardrails and content moderation by instead expanding the conversation, giving it somewhere else to travel than a dead end, a denial, or a generic apology that shuts down the conversation altogether.
